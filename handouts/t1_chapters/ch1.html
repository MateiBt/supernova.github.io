1.1.1 Planar Polar Coordinates $(r, \theta)$

For motion confined to a 2D plane, we use polar coordinates. A point $P$ is defined by its distance $r$ from the origin and its angle $\theta$ measured counterclockwise from a reference axis (usually the positive $x$-axis). Just like with Cartesian coordinates, we need two perpendicular unit vectors to describe the motion in polar coordinates.

Thus we define:

  1. $\hat{e}_r$: The radial unit vector, pointing directly away from the origin toward $P$.
  2. $\hat{e}_\theta$: The azimuthal (or transverse) unit vector, pointing perpendicular to $\hat{e}_r$ in the direction of increasing $\theta$.

Just like $\hat{i}$ and $\hat{j}$ point in directions of increasing $x$ and $y$ coordinates, $\hat{e}_r$ and $\hat{e}_\theta$ point in directions of increasing $r$ and $\theta$. We can express these in terms of the fixed Cartesian unit vectors ($\hat{i}, \hat{j}$):

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{e}_r &= \cos\theta \, \hat{i} + \sin\theta \, \hat{j} \\ \hat{e}_\theta &= -\sin\theta \, \hat{i} + \cos\theta \, \hat{j} \end{aligned} \]

Time Derivatives of Unit Vectors in Polar Coordinates

As the particle moves, $\theta$ changes with time ($\theta = \theta(t)$), so the unit vectors rotate. Using the chain rule, we differentiate them with respect to time $t$, noting that $\hat{i}$ and $\hat{j}$ are constant in time:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d\hat{e}_r}{dt} = \dot{\hat{e}}_r &= (-\sin\theta \cdot \dot{\theta}) \hat{i} + (\cos\theta \cdot \dot{\theta}) \hat{j} \\ &= \dot{\theta}(-\sin\theta \, \hat{i} + \cos\theta \, \hat{j}) \end{aligned} \]

Notice that the term in parentheses is exactly $\hat{e}_\theta$. Therefore:

\[ \dot{\hat{e}}_r = \dot{\theta} \hat{e}_\theta \]

Similarly, differentiating $\hat{e}_\theta$:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d\hat{e}_\theta}{dt} = \dot{\hat{e}}_\theta &= (-\cos\theta \cdot \dot{\theta}) \hat{i} - (\sin\theta \cdot \dot{\theta}) \hat{j} \\ &= -\dot{\theta}(\cos\theta \, \hat{i} + \sin\theta \, \hat{j}) \\ \dot{\hat{e}}_\theta &= -\dot{\theta} \hat{e}_r \end{aligned} \]
Idea: Geometric Shortcut for Rotating Unit Vectors

You never need to write out $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$ to find $\dot{\hat{e}}_r$ and $\dot{\hat{e}}_\theta$. Any unit vector has a fixed length of $1$, so as it rotates counterclockwise by an angle $d\theta$, its tip moves by a distance $1 \cdot d\theta$ perpendicular to itself in the direction of rotation: $\hat{e}_r$ tips toward $+\hat{e}_\theta$ ($\dot{\hat{e}}_r = \dot{\theta}\hat{e}_\theta$), while $\hat{e}_\theta$ tips inward toward $-\hat{e}_r$ ($\dot{\hat{e}}_\theta = -\dot{\theta}\hat{e}_r$). Equivalently, for any unit vector $\hat{u}$ rotating with angular velocity $\vec{\omega} = \dot{\theta}\hat{k}$, $\dot{\hat{u}} = \vec{\omega} \times \hat{u}$.

Velocity in Polar Coordinates

The position vector of the particle is simply its distance $r$ in the direction of $\hat{e}_r$:

\[ \vec{r} = r \hat{e}_r \]

To find the velocity vector $\vec{v}$, we take the time derivative of $\vec{r}$, applying the product rule:

\[ \vec{v} = \frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} = \dot{r} \hat{e}_r + r \dot{\hat{e}}_r \]

Substituting our derived expression for $\dot{\hat{e}}_r$:

\[ \vec{v} = \dot{r} \hat{e}_r + r\dot{\theta} \hat{e}_\theta \]

Here, $v_r = \dot{r}$ is the radial velocity (how fast the distance from the origin is changing), and $v_\theta = r\dot{\theta}$ is the transverse (or azimuthal) velocity (how fast the particle is sweeping across the angle).

Acceleration in Polar Coordinates

To find the acceleration vector $\vec{a}$, we differentiate the velocity vector $\vec{v}$, again using the product rule on both terms:

\[ \begin{aligned} \vec{a} = \frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} &= \frac{d}{dt}(\dot{r} \hat{e}_r) + \frac{d}{dt}(r\dot{\theta} \hat{e}_\theta) \\ &= (\ddot{r} \hat{e}_r + \dot{r} \dot{\hat{e}}_r) + (\dot{r}\dot{\theta} \hat{e}_\theta + r\ddot{\theta} \hat{e}_\theta + r\dot{\theta} \dot{\hat{e}}_\theta) \end{aligned} \]

Substitute $\dot{\hat{e}}_r = \dot{\theta} \hat{e}_\theta$ and $\dot{\hat{e}}_\theta = -\dot{\theta} \hat{e}_r$:

\[ \vec{a} = \ddot{r} \hat{e}_r + \dot{r}(\dot{\theta} \hat{e}_\theta) + \dot{r}\dot{\theta} \hat{e}_\theta + r\ddot{\theta} \hat{e}_\theta + r\dot{\theta}(-\dot{\theta} \hat{e}_r) \]

Grouping the $\hat{e}_r$ and $\hat{e}_\theta$ terms gives the fundamental acceleration equation in polar coordinates:

\[ \vec{a} = (\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2) \hat{e}_r + (r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}) \hat{e}_\theta \]

$a_r = \ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2$ represents the total radial acceleration. The $- r\dot{\theta}^2$ term is the familiar centripetal acceleration required to keep a body in a curved path.

$a_\theta = r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}$ represents the transverse acceleration. The $2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}$ term is the Coriolis acceleration, which arises when a particle moves radially while the reference frame rotates (half comes from the radial velocity vector $\dot{r}\hat{e}_r$ tipping toward $\hat{e}_\theta$, and the other half comes from the larger linear speed $(r+dr)\dot{\theta}$ required to maintain angular rate $\dot{\theta}$ at a larger radius).

Note: In a purely central force field (like gravity, where force is only along the radial direction), the transverse acceleration $a_\theta = 0$. As we will see in Section 1.2, setting $r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta} = \frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dt}(r^2\dot{\theta}) = 0$ leads directly to the conservation of angular momentum and to Kepler's Second Law.

1.1.2 Spherical Coordinates $(r, \theta, \phi)$ & Cylindrical Coordinates $(r, \phi, z)$

Spherical Coordinates $(r, \theta, \phi)$

This is the standard system used in astronomy (e.g., Right Ascension and Declination are forms of spherical coordinates).

  • $r$: Radial distance from the origin.
  • $\theta$: The polar angle (co-latitude or zenith angle), measured from the positive $z$-axis down ($0 \le \theta \le \pi$).
  • $\phi$: The azimuthal angle (longitude), measured counterclockwise in the $xy$-plane from the positive $x$-axis ($0 \le \phi \le 2\pi$).

The position vector is simply $\vec{r} = r \hat{e}_r$. The derivation of velocity and acceleration is similar to the one in 2D, but with three rotating unit vectors ($\hat{e}_r, \hat{e}_\theta, \hat{e}_\phi$), each pointing in the direction of increase of the corresponding coordinate. The results are:

\[ \vec{v} = \dot{r} \hat{e}_r + r\dot{\theta} \hat{e}_\theta + r\dot{\phi}\sin\theta \, \hat{e}_\phi \] \[ \begin{aligned} \vec{a} = {} & (\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2 - r\dot{\phi}^2\sin^2\theta) \hat{e}_r \\ & + (r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta} - r\dot{\phi}^2\sin\theta\cos\theta) \hat{e}_\theta \\ & + (r\ddot{\phi}\sin\theta + 2\dot{r}\dot{\phi}\sin\theta + 2r\dot{\theta}\dot{\phi}\cos\theta) \hat{e}_\phi \end{aligned} \]

Cylindrical Coordinates $(r, \phi, z)$

Useful for problems involving accretion disks or planetary rings. It is simply planar polar coordinates $(r, \phi)$ in the $xy$-plane, plus a Cartesian $z$ axis.

  • $r$: Radial distance from the $z$-axis (often denoted as $\rho$ or $s$ to distinguish from spherical $r$).
  • $\phi$: Azimuthal angle in the $xy$-plane.
  • $z$: Height above the $xy$-plane.

Because the $\hat{e}_z$ unit vector is fixed, its time derivative is zero ($\dot{\hat{e}}_z = 0$). We just add the $z$ components to the equations in polar coordinates:

\[ \vec{v} = \dot{r} \hat{e}_r + r\dot{\phi} \hat{e}_\phi + \dot{z} \hat{e}_z \] \[ \vec{a} = (\ddot{r} - r\dot{\phi}^2) \hat{e}_r + (r\ddot{\phi} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\phi}) \hat{e}_\phi + \ddot{z} \hat{e}_z \]
Worked Example 1.1: The Logarithmic Spiral Orbit in a Central Field

A test particle of mass $m$ moves in a planar central force field $\vec{F}(r) = F(r)\hat{e}_r$ centered at the origin $O$. Observations show that its trajectory is a logarithmic spiral $r(\theta) = r_0 e^{k\theta}$, where $r_0 > 0$ and $k \neq 0$ are constants. (a) Show that the angle $\gamma$ between the velocity vector $\vec{v}$ and the azimuthal unit vector $\hat{e}_\theta$ is constant everywhere. (b) Given that $a_\theta = 0$, find $r(t)$ and the central force law $F(r)$.

Solution:
(a) Since $\vec{v} = \dot{r}\hat{e}_r + r\dot{\theta}\hat{e}_\theta$, the tangent of the angle $\gamma$ between $\vec{v}$ and $\hat{e}_\theta$ is $\tan\gamma = \frac{v_r}{v_\theta} = \frac{\dot{r}}{r\dot{\theta}} = \frac{1}{r}\frac{dr}{d\theta}$. Differentiating $r(\theta) = r_0 e^{k\theta}$ gives $\frac{dr}{d\theta} = k r_0 e^{k\theta} = kr$, so $\tan\gamma = k = \text{constant}$.
(b) Since $a_\theta = \frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dt}(r^2\dot{\theta}) = 0$, specific angular momentum $h = r^2\dot{\theta}$ is constant. Substituting $\dot{\theta} = h/r^2$ into $\dot{r} = kr\dot{\theta}$ gives $\dot{r} = \frac{kh}{r}$. Integrating $r\,dr = kh\,dt$ yields $r(t) = \sqrt{r_0^2 + 2kht}$. Differentiating $\dot{r} = kh/r$ gives $\ddot{r} = -\frac{kh}{r^2}\dot{r} = -\frac{k^2 h^2}{r^3}$. Substituting $\ddot{r}$ and $r\dot{\theta}^2 = \frac{h^2}{r^3}$ into $a_r = \ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2$ yields:

\[ F(r) = m(\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2) = -\frac{m(1 + k^2)h^2}{r^3} \]

Thus, a logarithmic spiral trajectory requires an attractive inverse-cube central force $F(r) \propto -1/r^3$.

1.2 Conservation Laws in Newtonian Mechanics

1.2.1 Conservation of Linear & Angular Momentum

Let us consider a particle of mass $m$ (e.g., a planet) orbiting a much larger primary body of mass $M$ (e.g., a star) located at the origin of an inertial reference frame.

According to Newton's Second Law, the net force $\vec{F}$ acting on the particle relates to its acceleration $\vec{a} = \ddot{\vec{r}}$ by:

\[ \vec{F} = m\ddot{\vec{r}} = \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt} \]

where $\vec{p} = m\vec{v}$ is the linear momentum. In a central force field, the force vector is always aligned with the radial position vector $\vec{r}$. Mathematically, this means $\vec{F} = F(r)\hat{e}_r$, where $F(r)$ is a scalar function of distance (for gravity, $F(r) = -GMm/r^2$).

Torque and Angular Momentum

The angular momentum $\vec{L}$ of the particle with respect to the origin is defined as the cross product of its position vector and its linear momentum:

\[ \vec{L} = \vec{r} \times \vec{p} = m(\vec{r} \times \vec{v}) \]

To see how $\vec{L}$ changes over time, we take its time derivative using the product rule for cross products:

\[ \frac{d\vec{L}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt} (\vec{r} \times \vec{p}) = \left( \frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} \times \vec{p} \right) + \left( \vec{r} \times \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt} \right) \]

Let's evaluate these two terms:

  1. The first term is $(\vec{v} \times m\vec{v})$. Since any vector crossed with itself is zero ($\vec{v}$ is parallel to $m\vec{v}$), this term vanishes.
  2. The second term is $(\vec{r} \times \vec{F})$. This is the fundamental definition of torque ($\vec{\tau}$).

Therefore, the rate of change of angular momentum equals the net torque:

\[ \frac{d\vec{L}}{dt} = \vec{\tau} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F} \]

Application to Central Forces

Because we are dealing with a central force ($\vec{F} = F(r)\hat{e}_r$) and the position vector is purely radial ($\vec{r} = r\hat{e}_r$), the torque becomes:

\[ \vec{\tau} = (r\hat{e}_r) \times (F(r)\hat{e}_r) = rF(r) (\hat{e}_r \times \hat{e}_r) = \vec{0} \]

Since the torque is zero, the angular momentum vector must be a constant in time:

\[ \frac{d\vec{L}}{dt} = \vec{0} \implies \vec{L} = \text{constant vector} \]

Specific Angular Momentum ($\vec{h}$)

We define the specific angular momentum ($\vec{h}$), which is the angular momentum per unit mass:

\[ \vec{h} = \frac{\vec{L}}{m} = \vec{r} \times \vec{v} \]

Like $\vec{L}$, the specific angular momentum $\vec{h}$ is a conserved vector. Because $\vec{h}$ is always perpendicular to both $\vec{r}$ and $\vec{v}$, and its direction never changes, the vectors $\vec{r}$ and $\vec{v}$ must always lie in the same plane. All unperturbed two-body orbits are strictly planar. We will align our coordinate system such that $\vec{h}$ points along the $z$-axis: $\vec{h} = h\hat{k}$.

Kepler's 2nd Law

Kepler's 2nd Law states that the position vector of a planet sweeps out equal areas during equal intervals of time. In this section, we will derive it using Newtonian mechanics.[1]

We start by writing the specific angular momentum in polar coordinates, by using the formulas derived in Section 1.1: $\vec{r} = r\hat{e}_r$ and $\vec{v} = \dot{r}\hat{e}_r + r\dot{\theta}\hat{e}_\theta$.

\[ \begin{aligned} \vec{h} &= (r\hat{e}_r) \times (\dot{r}\hat{e}_r + r\dot{\theta}\hat{e}_\theta) \\ &= r\dot{r}(\hat{e}_r \times \hat{e}_r) + r^2\dot{\theta}(\hat{e}_r \times \hat{e}_\theta) \end{aligned} \]

Since $\hat{e}_r \times \hat{e}_r = \vec{0}$ and $\hat{e}_r \times \hat{e}_\theta = \hat{k}$ (the $z$-axis perpendicular to the plane), we get:

\[ \vec{h} = r^2\dot{\theta} \hat{k} \implies h = r^2\dot{\theta} = \text{constant} \]

Geometrically, as the radius vector $\vec{r}$ sweeps through an infinitesimal angle $d\theta$, it traces out an infinitesimal triangular area $dA = \frac{1}{2} r (r d\theta) = \frac{1}{2} r^2 d\theta$. Dividing by the time interval $dt$, we find the areal velocity:

\[ \frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{1}{2} r^2 \frac{d\theta}{dt} = \frac{1}{2} r^2 \dot{\theta} \]

Substituting $h = r^2\dot{\theta}$, we arrive at:

\[ \frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{h}{2} = \text{constant} \]

which concludes the derivation.

[1] Actually, Kepler's Laws have been used by Newton to come up with his theory for gravity, not the other way around. Kepler derived them empirically from planet ephemerides, almost 80 years before Newton came up with his theory.

1.2.2 Mechanical Energy in Gravitational Fields

Just as angular momentum is conserved due to the lack of torque, mechanical energy is conserved because gravity is a conservative force.

Work-Energy Theorem & Potential Energy

The work $W$ done by a force moving a particle from position $\vec{r}_1$ to $\vec{r}_2$ is the line integral of the force:

\[ W = \int_{\vec{r}_1}^{\vec{r}_2} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} \]

For a central force $\vec{F} = F(r)\hat{e}_r$, the dot product $\vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r}$ simplifies to $F(r)dr$. The work done depends only on the initial and final radial distances ($r_1$ and $r_2$), not the path taken. Therefore, the force is conservative, and we can define a potential energy function $U(r)$ such that the force is the negative gradient of the potential ($\vec{F} = -\nabla U$).

For Newtonian gravity, $F(r) = -\frac{GMm}{r^2}$. The gravitational potential energy is:

\[ U(r) = -\int F(r) \, dr = -\int \left(-\frac{GMm}{r^2}\right) dr = -\frac{GMm}{r} \]

(Note: We set the constant of integration to zero so that $U \to 0$ as $r \to \infty$.)

Specific Mechanical Energy ($\epsilon$)

The total mechanical energy $E$ of the system is the sum of kinetic energy ($K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$) and potential energy ($U$):

\[ E = K + U = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{GMm}{r} = \text{constant} \]

Just as we did with angular momentum, we divide by the particle's mass $m$ to define the specific mechanical energy ($\epsilon$):

\[ \epsilon = \frac{E}{m} = \frac{1}{2}v^2 - \frac{GM}{r} \]

This quantity remains constant everywhere along an unperturbed orbit.

Now recall that the squared velocity is $v^2 = \vec{v} \cdot \vec{v} = (\dot{r}\hat{e}_r + r\dot{\theta}\hat{e}_\theta) \cdot (\dot{r}\hat{e}_r + r\dot{\theta}\hat{e}_\theta) = \dot{r}^2 + r^2\dot{\theta}^2$. Substituting this into the energy equation yields:

\[ \epsilon = \frac{1}{2}(\dot{r}^2 + r^2\dot{\theta}^2) - \frac{GM}{r} \]

Now, substitute the relation for specific angular momentum ($\dot{\theta} = h/r^2$):

\[ \epsilon = \frac{1}{2}\dot{r}^2 + \frac{h^2}{2r^2} - \frac{GM}{r} \]

This is an important result. We have completely eliminated the angular variable $\theta$. The term $\frac{h^2}{2r^2}$ acts as a "centrifugal potential energy." We define the effective potential $U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)$:

\[ \begin{aligned} U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r) &= \frac{h^2}{2r^2} - \frac{GM}{r} \\ \epsilon &= \frac{1}{2}\dot{r}^2 + U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r) \end{aligned} \]
Idea: Reading Orbits Directly from the Effective Potential

Because kinetic energy $\frac{1}{2}\dot{r}^2 = \epsilon - U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)$ cannot be negative, a particle with specific angular momentum $h$ and specific energy $\epsilon$ can only exist where $\epsilon \ge U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)$:

  • Turning Points (Periapsis & Apoapsis): Setting $\dot{r} = 0$ ($\epsilon = U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)$) gives the quadratic equation $\epsilon r^2 + GMr - \frac{1}{2}h^2 = 0$, whose roots immediately give $r_p$ and $r_a$ without solving for the orbit's shape!
  • Circular Orbit ($\epsilon = \epsilon_{\min}$): At the bottom of the potential well, $\frac{dU_{\mathrm{eff}}}{dr} = -\frac{h^2}{r^3} + \frac{GM}{r^2} = 0$, giving $r_c = \frac{h^2}{GM}$ and $\epsilon_{\min} = -\frac{G^2 M^2}{2h^2} = -\frac{GM}{2r_c}$.
  • Bound vs. Unbound: For $\epsilon_{\min} \lt \epsilon \lt 0$, the radius oscillates between $r_p$ and $r_a$ (ellipse). For $\epsilon \ge 0$, the particle reflects once at $r_p$ and escapes to $r \to \infty$ (parabola if $\epsilon = 0$, hyperbola if $\epsilon > 0$).

Chapter 1 · Kinematics & Newtonian Gravitation in Central Fields

1.3 Gravitational Potential Theory

1.3.1 Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation & Gravitational Potential

Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation states that the attractive force $\vec{F}$ exerted by a point mass $M$ on another point mass $m$ located at a position vector $\vec{r}$ relative to $M$ is:

\[ \vec{F} = -\frac{GMm}{r^2} \hat{e}_r \]

where $G \approx 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m}^3\text{kg}^{-1}\text{s}^{-2}$ is the universal gravitational constant, and $\hat{e}_r$ is the radial unit vector pointing from $M$ to $m$.

Gravitational Field Vector ($\vec{g}$)

Instead of talking about the force on a specific test mass $m$, it is more useful to describe the gravitational field (or gravitational acceleration) generated by $M$ at any point in space. We define this field $\vec{g}(\vec{r})$ as the force per unit mass:

\[ \vec{g}(\vec{r}) = \frac{\vec{F}}{m} = -\frac{GM}{r^2} \hat{e}_r \]

Gravitational Potential ($\Phi$)

As proven in Section 1.2, gravity is a conservative force. Therefore, the gravitational field can be expressed as the negative gradient of a scalar gravitational potential $\Phi(r)$. The potential is the potential energy $U(r)$ per unit test mass:

\[ \Phi(r) = \frac{U(r)}{m} = -\frac{GM}{r} \]

By definition, $\vec{g} = -\nabla \Phi$. In spherical coordinates with spherical symmetry, the gradient is simply the radial derivative:

\[ \vec{g} = -\frac{d\Phi}{dr} \hat{e}_r = -\frac{d}{dr}\left(-\frac{GM}{r}\right) \hat{e}_r = -\frac{GM}{r^2} \hat{e}_r \]

Working with the scalar potential $\Phi$ is mathematically much simpler than working with the vector field $\vec{g}$, especially when summing the contributions of multiple masses (since scalars simply add: $\Phi_{\mathrm{total}} = \sum \Phi_i$).

1.3.2 Shell Theorem & Spherical Distributions

For extended continuous bodies, we replace the mass $M$ with a volume integral over the density distribution $\rho(\vec{r}')$:

\[ \Phi(\vec{r}) = -G \iiint \frac{\rho(\vec{r}')}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|} \, dV' \]

The integral above is a powerful tool. It allows one to find the potential at any point in space for any distribution of mass. Having expressed the potential, it is quite easy to find the field and then, the force.

In terms of notation, $dV'$ is the volume element, $\rho$ represents the mass distribution that causes the field; the prime notation in the position vectors is used to mark the positions of points in the mass distribution, while the un-primed $\vec{r}$ is the position vector of the point where the potential is calculated. Basically, the denominator is just the distance between a point in the mass distribution and wherever you want to compute the potential.

However, evaluating this 3D integral can be quite difficult for certain mass distributions. Sometimes another tool is more convenient, Gauss's Law for Gravity, which relates the gravitational flux through a closed surface to the mass enclosed within it:

\[ \oint_S \vec{g} \cdot d\vec{A} = -4\pi G M_{\mathrm{encl}} \]

Gauss's Law is also available for any mass distribution and any point in space, but it is usually practical only in situations where the mass distribution is symmetric.

The Shell Theorem

Consider a thin, uniform spherical shell of mass $M$ and radius $R$. By drawing a spherical Gaussian surface of radius $r$ concentric with the shell, we discover two profound truths that greatly simplify celestial mechanics:

  1. Outside the shell ($r > R$): The enclosed mass is $M$. Gauss's law gives $g(4\pi r^2) = -4\pi G M$, so $\vec{g} = -\frac{GM}{r^2}\hat{e}_r$.
    Conclusion: A spherically symmetric body affects external objects gravitationally as though all of its mass were concentrated at its exact center.
  2. Inside the shell ($r \lt R$): The enclosed mass is $0$. Gauss's law gives $g(4\pi r^2) = 0$, so $\vec{g} = \vec{0}$.
    Conclusion: Inside a uniform spherical shell, the gravitational field is strictly zero everywhere. Consequently, the potential $\Phi$ inside the shell is constant and equals its value at the surface: $\Phi_{\mathrm{inside}} = -\frac{GM}{R}$.

The Uniform Solid Sphere

Now consider a planet modeled as a uniform solid sphere of mass $M$ and radius $R$ with constant density $\rho = \frac{M}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3}$.

  • For external points ($r \ge R$): By the Shell Theorem, the sphere acts as a point mass.
    \[ g_{\mathrm{out}}(r) = -\frac{GM}{r^2}, \qquad \Phi_{\mathrm{out}}(r) = -\frac{GM}{r} \]
  • For internal points ($r \lt R$): A test particle at radius $r$ only feels gravity from the mass interior to its radius. The outer shells exert no net force. The enclosed mass is $M(r) = M(r/R)^3$.
    \[ g_{\mathrm{in}}(r) = -\frac{G M(r)}{r^2} = -\frac{G M (r/R)^3}{r^2} = -\frac{GM}{R^3} r \]
    Notice that gravity inside a uniform planet increases linearly with distance from the center.
    To find the internal potential $\Phi_{\mathrm{in}}(r)$, we integrate $g_{\mathrm{in}}$ and match the boundary condition at the surface ($r=R$):
    \[ \Phi_{\mathrm{in}}(r) = -\int g_{\mathrm{in}}(r) \, dr = \frac{GM}{2R^3} r^2 + C \]
    At $r=R$, $\Phi_{\mathrm{in}}(R) = \frac{GM}{2R} + C = -\frac{GM}{R} \implies C = -\frac{3GM}{2R}$.
    \[ \Phi_{\mathrm{in}}(r) = -\frac{GM}{2R^3} (3R^2 - r^2) \]
Idea: Superposition Trick for Off-Center Spherical Cavities

Because the internal gravitational field $\vec{g}_{\mathrm{in}}(\vec{r}) = -\frac{4}{3}\pi G\rho\,\vec{r}$ is linear in the position vector $\vec{r}$, if a spherical cavity centered at $\vec{a}$ is hollowed out of a uniform planet of density $\rho$, we can model the hollowed planet as a complete sphere of density $+\rho$ centered at $O$ plus a smaller sphere of negative density $-\rho$ centered at $\vec{a}$. Inside the cavity, a point at position $\vec{r}$ relative to $O$ is at $\vec{r} - \vec{a}$ relative to the cavity's center, so the net field inside the cavity is: \[ \vec{g}_{\mathrm{cav}}(\vec{r}) = -\frac{4}{3}\pi G\rho\,\vec{r} - \frac{4}{3}\pi G(-\rho)(\vec{r} - \vec{a}) = -\frac{4}{3}\pi G\rho\,\vec{a} = \text{constant!} \] The gravitational field inside any spherical cavity carved inside a uniform sphere is completely uniform everywhere inside the cavity!

Gravitational Binding Energy ($U$)

The gravitational binding energy is the total energy required to completely disassemble a celestial body into point masses at infinity. We calculate it by assembling the sphere shell by shell from the inside out. Let the current assembled mass be $M(r) = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \rho$. The work $dU$ required to bring the next shell of mass $dm = 4\pi r^2 \rho \, dr$ from infinity to the surface $r$ is:

\[ \begin{aligned} dU = \Phi(r) \, dm = -\frac{GM(r)}{r} dm &= -\frac{G \left(\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \rho\right) (4\pi r^2 \rho \, dr)}{r} \\ &= -\frac{16}{3}\pi^2 G \rho^2 r^4 \, dr \end{aligned} \]

Integrating from the center ($r=0$) to the total radius ($r=R$) and substituting $\rho = \frac{3M}{4\pi R^3}$ back into the equation:

\[ \begin{aligned} U = \int_0^R -\frac{16}{3}\pi^2 G \rho^2 r^4 \, dr &= -\frac{16}{3}\pi^2 G \rho^2 \frac{R^5}{5} \\ &= -\frac{16}{3}\pi^2 G \left( \frac{9M^2}{16\pi^2 R^6} \right) \frac{R^5}{5} = -\frac{3GM^2}{5R} \end{aligned} \]
IOAA Note: The factor of $3/5$ is specific to a uniform sphere. Real stars are denser in the core, making their binding energy more negative. In astrophysics problems, binding energy is often generalized as $U = -\alpha \frac{GM^2}{R}$, where $\alpha = 3/5$ for uniform planets, $\alpha = 6/7$ for convective stars/white dwarfs ($n = 1.5$ polytrope), and $\alpha = 3/2$ for radiative stars like the Sun ($n = 3$ polytrope).

1.3.3 The Virial Theorem for Bound Systems

While two-body systems are solved exactly (Chapter 2), systems containing $N$ interacting masses (like a star cluster or a galaxy) are chaotic. However, their global properties are strictly governed by the Virial Theorem.

Consider a system of $N$ particles with masses $m_i$ and position vectors $\vec{r}_i$. We define a scalar quantity called the moment of inertia $I$:

\[ I = \sum_{i=1}^N m_i r_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^N m_i (\vec{r}_i \cdot \vec{r}_i) \]

Taking the first and second time derivatives, applying the product rule:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{dI}{dt} &= 2 \sum_{i=1}^N m_i (\vec{r}_i \cdot \dot{\vec{r}}_i) = 2 \sum_{i=1}^N m_i (\vec{r}_i \cdot \vec{v}_i) \\ \frac{d^2 I}{dt^2} &= 2 \sum_{i=1}^N m_i (\vec{v}_i \cdot \vec{v}_i + \vec{r}_i \cdot \dot{\vec{v}}_i) \end{aligned} \]

Recognize that $\vec{v}_i \cdot \vec{v}_i = v_i^2$, and $m_i \dot{\vec{v}}_i = m_i \vec{a}_i = \vec{F}_i$ (the total force on particle $i$):

\[ \frac{1}{2} \frac{d^2 I}{dt^2} = \sum_{i=1}^N m_i v_i^2 + \sum_{i=1}^N \vec{r}_i \cdot \vec{F}_i \]

Let's evaluate the two sums on the right side:

  1. Kinetic Energy Term: We know kinetic energy is $K_i = \frac{1}{2} m_i v_i^2$. Therefore, the first sum is exactly twice the total kinetic energy of the system ($2K$).
  2. Virial Term (Force Sum): The total force on particle $i$ is the sum of gravitational forces from all other particles $j$: $\vec{F}_i = \sum_{j \ne i} \vec{F}_{ij}$. By Newton's Third Law, $\vec{F}_{ji} = -\vec{F}_{ij}$. We can pair up the terms for any two interacting particles $i$ and $j$:
    \[ \begin{aligned} \vec{r}_i \cdot \vec{F}_{ij} + \vec{r}_j \cdot \vec{F}_{ji} &= (\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_j) \cdot \vec{F}_{ij} \\ &= -\frac{G m_i m_j}{|\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_j|^3} |\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_j|^2 = -\frac{G m_i m_j}{|\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_j|} = U_{ij} \end{aligned} \]
    This is exactly the gravitational potential energy $U_{ij}$ between the two particles! Summing over all unique pairs gives the total potential energy $U$.

Plugging these back into our second derivative equation yields the generalized Virial Theorem (the Lagrange–Jacobi Identity):

\[ \frac{1}{2} \frac{d^2 I}{dt^2} = 2K + U \]

The Steady-State Virial Theorem

If a star cluster or galaxy is in a stable, bound, dynamically relaxed state (it is neither collapsing nor exploding), its moment of inertia $I$ fluctuates around a constant mean value. Therefore, the time-averaged second derivative $\langle \ddot{I} \rangle$ is zero. This yields the fundamental equation of statistical celestial mechanics:

\[ 2\langle K \rangle + \langle U \rangle = 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad E = -\langle K \rangle = \frac{1}{2}\langle U \rangle \]

This theorem is heavily tested in IOAA. It allows you to estimate the total mass of a galaxy cluster just by measuring the velocity dispersion (kinetic energy) of its galaxies and its approximate radius (potential energy), which historically provided the first evidence for Dark Matter.

Worked Example 1.3: Cluster Virial Mass & 3D Velocity Dispersion (IOAA 2024 Theory)

Spectroscopic observations of a spherical galaxy cluster of radius $R$ measure the line-of-sight radial velocity dispersion $\sigma_r = \sqrt{\langle (v_{i,r} - \bar{v}_r)^2 \rangle}$ of $N \gg 1$ member galaxies. Assuming isotropic galaxy velocities and a uniform spatial mass distribution of total mass $M$, prove that the virial mass of the cluster is $M = \frac{5R\sigma_r^2}{G}$.

Solution:
In the cluster rest frame, each galaxy has 3D velocity $\vec{u}_i = (u_{i,x}, u_{i,y}, u_{i,z})$ where $u_{i,z} = v_{i,r} - \bar{v}_r$ is along the line of sight. By 3D isotropy, $\langle u_i^2 \rangle = \langle u_{i,x}^2 \rangle + \langle u_{i,y}^2 \rangle + \langle u_{i,z}^2 \rangle = 3\sigma_r^2$. Thus the total kinetic energy is $\langle K \rangle = \frac{1}{2}M\langle u_i^2 \rangle = \frac{3}{2}M\sigma_r^2$. For a uniform sphere of mass $M$ and radius $R$, $\langle U \rangle = -\frac{3}{5}\frac{GM^2}{R}$. Applying the Virial Theorem $2\langle K \rangle + \langle U \rangle = 0$ gives $3M\sigma_r^2 = \frac{3}{5}\frac{GM^2}{R} \implies M = \frac{5R\sigma_r^2}{G}$.

Hint: Integrate $M(r) = \int_0^r 4\pi r'^2\rho(r')\,dr'$. Set $M(R) = M$ to find $\rho_c = \frac{3M}{\pi R^3}$, and integrate $U = -\int_0^R \frac{GM(r)}{r} 4\pi r^2\rho(r)\,dr$ using the dimensionless variable $x = r/R$.
Full Solution:

(a) Integrating the enclosed mass $M(r)$ from $0$ to $r$:

\[ M(r) = \int_0^r 4\pi r'^2 \rho_c\left(1 - \frac{r'}{R}\right)dr' = 4\pi\rho_c r^3\left(\frac{1}{3} - \frac{r}{4R}\right) \]

At $r = R$, $M = \frac{\pi}{3}\rho_c R^3 \implies \rho_c = \frac{3M}{\pi R^3}$ (four times the mean density!). Substituting $\rho_c$ gives:

\[ M(r) = M\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^3\left(4 - \frac{3r}{R}\right), \qquad g(r) = \frac{GM(r)}{r^2} = \frac{GM}{R^2}\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)\left(4 - \frac{3r}{R}\right) \]

(b) Differentiating $g(x) \propto 4x - 3x^2$ (where $x = r/R$) gives $4 - 6x = 0 \implies r_{\max} = \frac{2}{3}R$, where $\frac{g(r_{\max})}{g(R)} = \frac{2}{3}\left(4 - 2\right) = \frac{4}{3}$.

(c) With $dm = 4\pi r^2\rho(r)\,dr = 12M x^2(1-x)\,dx$ and $M(x) = Mx^3(4-3x)$:

\[ U = -\frac{GM^2}{R}\int_0^1 12x^4(4 - 3x)(1 - x)\,dx = -12\frac{GM^2}{R}\left(\frac{4}{5} - \frac{7}{6} + \frac{3}{7}\right) = -\frac{26}{35}\frac{GM^2}{R} \]

Thus $\alpha = \frac{26}{35} \approx 0.743 > \frac{3}{5} = 0.600$, confirming that central condensation increases binding energy.

Problem 1.8 · White Dwarf Equilibrium & Chandrasekhar Limit (APhO 2012, Theory Q2) [4 pts]

A white dwarf star of mass $M$ and radius $R$ (modeled as a uniform sphere with gravitational potential energy $E_G = -\frac{3}{5}\frac{GM^2}{R}$) is supported against gravity by electron degeneracy kinetic energy $E_e$.

  1. In the non-relativistic regime, the total kinetic energy of the degenerate electrons is $E_e = \frac{C_1 M^{5/3}}{R^2}$, where $C_1$ is a constant. Write down the total energy $E(R) = E_e(R) + E_G(R)$, and find the equilibrium radius $R_{\mathrm{wd}}(M)$ where $\frac{dE}{dR} = 0$. How does $R_{\mathrm{wd}}$ scale with mass $M$?
  2. In the extreme ultrarelativistic limit ($v \to c$), the electron kinetic energy scales as $E_e = \frac{C_2 M^{4/3}}{R}$, where $C_2$ is a constant. Write down $E(R)$ in this limit, deduce the critical Chandrasekhar mass $M_{\mathrm{Ch}}$ in terms of $C_2$ and $G$, and explain what happens if $M > M_{\mathrm{Ch}}$.
Hint: Differentiate $E(R)$ with respect to $R$ and set $dE/dR = 0$. Notice that in part (b), both $E_e$ and $E_G$ scale as $1/R$!
Full Solution:

(a) In the non-relativistic regime, the total energy is $E(R) = \frac{C_1 M^{5/3}}{R^2} - \frac{3}{5}\frac{GM^2}{R}$. Setting $\frac{dE}{dR} = 0$ at $R = R_{\mathrm{wd}}$:

\[ \frac{dE}{dR} = -\frac{2C_1 M^{5/3}}{R_{\mathrm{wd}}^3} + \frac{3GM^2}{5R_{\mathrm{wd}}^2} = 0 \implies R_{\mathrm{wd}} = \frac{10 C_1}{3G}M^{-1/3} \]

Notice that this is equivalent to the Virial Theorem $2E_e + E_G = 0$, and shows that more massive white dwarfs are smaller ($R_{\mathrm{wd}} \propto M^{-1/3}$)!

(b) In the ultrarelativistic limit, both $E_e$ and $E_G$ scale as $R^{-1}$:

\[ E(R) = \left(C_2 M^{4/3} - \frac{3}{5}GM^2\right)\frac{1}{R} \]

Equilibrium ($dE/dR = 0$, or the relativistic Virial Theorem $E_e + E_G = 0$) is only possible at the unique critical mass where the coefficient of $1/R$ vanishes: $M_{\mathrm{Ch}} = \left(\frac{5C_2}{3G}\right)^{3/2}$. If $M > M_{\mathrm{Ch}}$, the bracket is negative and $E(R) \to -\infty$ as $R \to 0$, so degeneracy pressure cannot halt gravitational collapse!

Problem 1.9 · Radial Free-Fall Collapse Time (IPhO 2012, Short Problem S3) [4 pts]

Two point particles, one of large mass $M$ and one of negligible mass $m \ll M$, are initially held at rest at separation $r_0$ and released at $t = 0$ so that $m$ falls radially into $M$.

  1. Using conservation of specific mechanical energy $\epsilon$, show that the collapse time $t_{\mathrm{fall}}$ to reach $r = 0$ is given by $t_{\mathrm{fall}} = \int_0^{r_0} \frac{dr}{\sqrt{2GM\left(\frac{1}{r} - \frac{1}{r_0}\right)}}$.
  2. Evaluate this integral exactly using the substitution $r = r_0\cos^2\psi$, and verify that your result matches half the period of a degenerate Keplerian ellipse of semi-major axis $a = r_0/2$.
Hint: With $r = r_0\cos^2\psi$, we have $dr = -2r_0\cos\psi\sin\psi\,d\psi$ and $\sqrt{\frac{r}{r_0 - r}} = \frac{\cos\psi}{\sin\psi}$. Use $\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^2\psi\,d\psi = \frac{\pi}{4}$.
Full Solution:

(a) Since the particle starts from rest at $r_0$ with $h = 0$, $\epsilon = \frac{1}{2}\dot{r}^2 - \frac{GM}{r} = -\frac{GM}{r_0}$. Solving for $\dot{r} = \frac{dr}{dt} \lt 0$ gives:

\[ \frac{dr}{dt} = -\sqrt{2GM\left(\frac{1}{r} - \frac{1}{r_0}\right)} \implies t_{\mathrm{fall}} = \int_0^{r_0} \sqrt{\frac{r_0 r}{2GM(r_0 - r)}}\,dr \]

(b) Substituting $r = r_0\cos^2\psi$ ($\psi: \frac{\pi}{2} \to 0$, $dr = -2r_0\cos\psi\sin\psi\,d\psi$) transforms the integral into:

\[ t_{\mathrm{fall}} = \sqrt{\frac{r_0^3}{2GM}}\int_0^{\pi/2} 2\cos^2\psi\,d\psi = \sqrt{\frac{r_0^3}{2GM}}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \pi\sqrt{\frac{r_0^3}{8GM}} \]

Treating the straight-line fall as a degenerate ellipse of apoapsis $r_a = r_0$, periapsis $r_p = 0$, and semi-major axis $a = \frac{r_0 + 0}{2} = \frac{r_0}{2}$, half the Keplerian period is $\frac{1}{2}T = \pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{GM}} = \pi\sqrt{\frac{r_0^3}{8GM}}$, in exact agreement!

Problem 1.11 · [A] Generalized Power-Law Virial Theorem & Negative Heat Capacity [5 pts]

Suppose an isolated $N$-body system interacts via a central power-law pair potential $U_{ij} = \kappa m_i m_j |\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_j|^n$, where $n \neq 0$ is a real exponent ($n = -1$ for Newtonian gravity, $n = +2$ for a 3D harmonic oscillator).

  1. By evaluating the virial force sum $\sum_i \vec{r}_i \cdot \vec{F}_i$, prove the generalized Lagrange–Jacobi identity $\frac{1}{2}\ddot{I} = 2K - nU$ and the steady-state generalized Virial Theorem $2\langle K \rangle = n\langle U \rangle$.
  2. Express the total energy $E = \langle K \rangle + \langle U \rangle$ in terms of $\langle K \rangle$ alone. For what range of exponents $n$ does a bound system exhibit negative heat capacity ($\frac{dE}{d\langle K \rangle} \lt 0$)? What happens in the critical case $n = -2$?
Hint: For pair distance $r_{ij} = |\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_j|$, the force on $i$ due to $j$ is $\vec{F}_{ij} = -\nabla_i U_{ij} = -n\kappa m_i m_j r_{ij}^{n-2}(\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_j) = -\frac{n U_{ij}}{r_{ij}^2}(\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_j)$.
Full Solution:

(a) Since $\vec{F}_{ij} = -\nabla_i U_{ij} = -n\kappa m_i m_j |\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_j|^{n-2}(\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_j)$, pairing $(i,j)$ via Newton's Third Law gives:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^N \vec{r}_i \cdot \vec{F}_i = \sum_{i \lt j} (\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_j) \cdot \vec{F}_{ij} = -n\sum_{i \lt j} \kappa m_i m_j |\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_j|^n = -nU \]

Substituting into $\frac{1}{2}\ddot{I} = 2K + \sum_i \vec{r}_i \cdot \vec{F}_i$ gives $\frac{1}{2}\ddot{I} = 2K - nU$. In a steady state ($\langle \ddot{I} \rangle = 0$), we obtain $2\langle K \rangle = n\langle U \rangle$.

(b) Substituting $\langle U \rangle = \frac{2}{n}\langle K \rangle$ into $E = \langle K \rangle + \langle U \rangle$ gives:

\[ E = \left(1 + \frac{2}{n}\right)\langle K \rangle = \left(\frac{n + 2}{2}\right)\langle U \rangle \]

Thus $\frac{dE}{d\langle K \rangle} = 1 + \frac{2}{n} \lt 0$ whenever $-2 \lt n \lt 0$ (which includes Newtonian gravity $n = -1$!). In the borderline case $n = -2$ (an inverse-cube force $F \propto -1/r^3$), $\frac{1}{2}\ddot{I} = 2(K + U) = 2E$, so bound steady states can only exist at the single fine-tuned energy $E = 0$!
