2.1 Two-Body Problem Reduction

The foundational model of orbital mechanics is the isolated gravitational interaction of two point masses (or spherically symmetric bodies by the Shell Theorem), $m_1$ and $m_2$. By exploiting the symmetries of Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation, the coupled six-degree-of-freedom motion of two interacting bodies can be decomposed into the trivial uniform motion of their common center of mass and the three-degree-of-freedom motion of a single fictitious particle in a static central field.

2.1.1 Center of Mass (Barycenter) Frame

Ok, now let's just consider two point masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ located at position vectors $\vec{r}_1$ and $\vec{r}_2$ with respect to an arbitrary inertial reference frame. The relative separation vector pointing from $m_2$ (often chosen as the primary body) to $m_1$ (the secondary body) is defined as:

\begin{equation} \vec{r} = \vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2, \qquad r = |\vec{r}| = |\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2| \end{equation}

with the corresponding radial unit vector pointing from $m_2$ toward $m_1$ given by $\hat{r} = \frac{\vec{r}}{r}$.

By Newton's Second Law and Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation, the equations of motion for the two masses in the inertial frame are:

\begin{align} m_1 \ddot{\vec{r}}_1 &= -\frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^2}\hat{r} = -\frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^3}(\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2) \label{eq:m1_inertial} \\ m_2 \ddot{\vec{r}}_2 &= +\frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^2}\hat{r} = +\frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^3}(\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2) \label{eq:m2_inertial} \end{align}

Adding Equations $\eqref{eq:m1_inertial}$ and $\eqref{eq:m2_inertial}$ yields zero net internal force on the system:

\begin{equation} m_1 \ddot{\vec{r}}_1 + m_2 \ddot{\vec{r}}_2 = \vec{0} \implies \frac{d^2}{dt^2}\left(m_1 \vec{r}_1 + m_2 \vec{r}_2\right) = \vec{0} \end{equation}

We define the position vector of the center of mass (barycenter) $\vec{R}_{\mathrm{cm}}$ as the mass-weighted average of the individual position vectors:

\begin{equation} \vec{R}_{\mathrm{cm}} \equiv \frac{m_1 \vec{r}_1 + m_2 \vec{r}_2}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{m_1 \vec{r}_1 + m_2 \vec{r}_2}{M} \end{equation}

where $M = m_1 + m_2$ denotes the total mass of the binary system. Because $\ddot{\vec{R}}_{\mathrm{cm}} = \vec{0}$, the barycenter moves at a constant velocity $\vec{V}_{\mathrm{cm}} = \dot{\vec{R}}_{\mathrm{cm}} = \mathrm{const}$, and therefore constitutes a valid inertial reference frame.

Transforming into the barycentric reference frame (where the origin is fixed at the center of mass, $\vec{R}_{\mathrm{cm}} = \vec{0}$), the position vectors of the individual bodies $\vec{r}_1' = \vec{r}_1 - \vec{R}_{\mathrm{cm}}$ and $\vec{r}_2' = \vec{r}_2 - \vec{R}_{\mathrm{cm}}$ satisfy the linear system:

\begin{equation} m_1 \vec{r}_1' + m_2 \vec{r}_2' = \vec{0} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \vec{r}_1' - \vec{r}_2' = \vec{r} \end{equation}

Solving this linear system expresses each body's barycentric position vector purely in terms of the relative separation vector $\vec{r}$:

\begin{align} \vec{r}_1' &= \left(\frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\right)\vec{r} = \frac{m_2}{M}\vec{r} \\ \vec{r}_2' &= -\left(\frac{m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\right)\vec{r} = -\frac{m_1}{M}\vec{r} \end{align}

Physical Consequences of the Barycentric Decomposition:

  • Opposite Phase: The vectors $\vec{r}_1'$ and $\vec{r}_2'$ are strictly anti-parallel ($\vec{r}_1' / r_1' = -\vec{r}_2' / r_2'$), meaning the two bodies always lie on opposite sides of the barycenter along a common line passing through it.
  • Inverse Mass Scaling: The distances from the barycenter satisfy $m_1 r_1' = m_2 r_2'$ and $r_1' + r_2' = r$, so the more massive body remains proportionally closer to the center of mass.
  • Homothetic Orbits: Once the relative trajectory $\vec{r}(t)$ is found, the individual barycentric orbits $\vec{r}_1'(t)$ and $\vec{r}_2'(t)$ are geometrically similar conic sections with the exact same eccentricity $e$, scaled by factors of $m_2/M$ and $m_1/M$, respectively.

2.1.2 Reduced Mass & Equation of Relative Motion

To isolate the relative motion $\vec{r}(t)$, divide the inertial equations of motion $\eqref{eq:m1_inertial}$ and $\eqref{eq:m2_inertial}$ by $m_1$ and $m_2$, respectively:

\begin{equation} \ddot{\vec{r}}_1 = -\frac{G m_2}{r^2}\hat{r}, \qquad \ddot{\vec{r}}_2 = +\frac{G m_1}{r^2}\hat{r} \end{equation}

Subtracting $\ddot{\vec{r}}_2$ from $\ddot{\vec{r}}_1$ yields the differential equation governing the separation vector $\vec{r} = \vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2$:

\begin{equation} \ddot{\vec{r}} = \ddot{\vec{r}}_1 - \ddot{\vec{r}}_2 = -\frac{G(m_1 + m_2)}{r^2}\hat{r} \end{equation}

We introduce two fundamental parameters to cast this equation into standard mechanical and astrodynamical forms:

  1. The Reduced Mass ($\mu$): Defined as the harmonic product of the two masses:
    \begin{equation} \mu \equiv \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{m_1 m_2}{M} \implies \frac{1}{\mu} = \frac{1}{m_1} + \frac{1}{m_2} \end{equation}
  2. The Standard Gravitational Parameter ($\mu_0$): Defined as the product of the gravitational constant and the total system mass:
    \begin{equation} \mu_0 \equiv G(m_1 + m_2) = G M \end{equation}

Multiplying the relative acceleration equation by the reduced mass $\mu$ gives the Newtonian force form of the relative two-body problem:

\begin{equation} \mu \ddot{\vec{r}} = -\frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^2}\hat{r} \Longleftrightarrow \ddot{\vec{r}} = -\frac{\mu_0}{r^2}\hat{r} \end{equation}

Thus, the relative motion of two bodies with masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ is mathematically identical to the motion of a single fictitious particle of mass $\mu$ at position $\vec{r}$ orbiting a stationary central mass $M = m_1 + m_2$ fixed at the origin.

Total Energy and Angular Momentum in the Barycentric Frame:

In the center-of-mass frame ($\vec{R}_{\mathrm{cm}} = \vec{0}$), the total orbital angular momentum $\vec{L}_{\mathrm{tot}}$ and total mechanical energy $E_{\mathrm{tot}}$ of the two-body system simplify directly into the properties of the reduced-mass particle:

  • Total Angular Momentum: Substituting $\vec{r}_1' = \frac{m_2}{M}\vec{r}$ and $\vec{r}_2' = -\frac{m_1}{M}\vec{r}$ into $\vec{L}_{\mathrm{tot}} = m_1(\vec{r}_1' \times \dot{\vec{r}}_1') + m_2(\vec{r}_2' \times \dot{\vec{r}}_2')$ gives:
    \begin{equation} \vec{L}_{\mathrm{tot}} = \left[m_1\left(\frac{m_2}{M}\right)^2 + m_2\left(\frac{m_1}{M}\right)^2\right](\vec{r} \times \dot{\vec{r}}) = \frac{m_1 m_2(m_2 + m_1)}{M^2}(\vec{r} \times \vec{v}) = \mu(\vec{r} \times \vec{v}) = \mu \vec{h} \end{equation}
  • Total Mechanical Energy: Similarly, substituting $\dot{\vec{r}}_1'$ and $\dot{\vec{r}}_2'$ into the total kinetic and potential energy yields:
    \begin{equation} E_{\mathrm{tot}} = \frac{1}{2}m_1 |\dot{\vec{r}}_1'|^2 + \frac{1}{2}m_2 |\dot{\vec{r}}_2'|^2 - \frac{G m_1 m_2}{r} = \frac{1}{2}\mu v^2 - \frac{G M \mu}{r} = \mu\left(\frac{1}{2}v^2 - \frac{\mu_0}{r}\right) = \mu \epsilon \end{equation}

2.2 Conserved Vectors of the Motion

Because the equation of relative motion $\ddot{\vec{r}} = -\frac{\mu_0}{r^2}\hat{r}$ depends only on the mass sum $\mu_0 = G(m_1 + m_2)$, it is standard in celestial mechanics to work with specific (per unit reduced mass $\mu$) conserved quantities.

2.2.1 Specific Angular Momentum Vector ($\vec{h}$)

The specific relative angular momentum vector $\vec{h}$ is defined as the cross product of the relative position vector $\vec{r}$ and relative velocity vector $\vec{v} = \dot{\vec{r}}$:

\begin{equation} \vec{h} \equiv \frac{\vec{L}_{\mathrm{tot}}}{\mu} = \vec{r} \times \vec{v} = \vec{r} \times \dot{\vec{r}} \end{equation}

Proof of Conservation and Planar Motion:

Differentiating $\vec{h}$ with respect to time $t$ and applying the product rule for cross products yields:

\begin{equation} \frac{d\vec{h}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}(\vec{r} \times \dot{\vec{r}}) = (\dot{\vec{r}} \times \dot{\vec{r}}) + (\vec{r} \times \ddot{\vec{r}}) \end{equation}

The first term vanishes identically because the cross product of any vector with itself is zero ($\dot{\vec{r}} \times \dot{\vec{r}} = \vec{0}$). Substituting the equation of motion $\ddot{\vec{r}} = -\frac{\mu_0}{r^3}\vec{r}$ into the second term gives:

\begin{equation} \frac{d\vec{h}}{dt} = \vec{r} \times \left(-\frac{\mu_0}{r^3}\vec{r}\right) = -\frac{\mu_0}{r^3}(\vec{r} \times \vec{r}) = \vec{0} \implies \vec{h} = \mathrm{const} \end{equation}

Because $\vec{h} = \vec{r} \times \vec{v}$ is strictly constant in both magnitude and direction, taking the scalar dot product of $\vec{r}$ and $\vec{v}$ with $\vec{h}$ at any instant $t$ yields:

\begin{equation} \vec{r} \cdot \vec{h} = \vec{r} \cdot (\vec{r} \times \vec{v}) = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \vec{v} \cdot \vec{h} = \vec{v} \cdot (\vec{r} \times \vec{v}) = 0 \end{equation}

Consequently, the position vector $\vec{r}$ and velocity vector $\vec{v}$ are forever perpendicular to the fixed direction of $\vec{h}$. All unperturbed two-body orbits are strictly planar, confined to the invariant orbital plane passing through the barycenter with normal unit vector $\hat{k} = \vec{h}/h$.

In planar polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ within the orbital plane, where $\vec{r} = r\hat{r}$ and $\vec{v} = \dot{r}\hat{r} + r\dot{\theta}\hat{\theta}$, the specific angular momentum simplifies to:

\begin{equation} \vec{h} = (r\hat{r}) \times (\dot{r}\hat{r} + r\dot{\theta}\hat{\theta}) = r^2\dot{\theta}(\hat{r} \times \hat{\theta}) = r^2\dot{\theta}\hat{k} \implies h = r^2\dot{\theta} = 2\frac{dA}{dt} = \mathrm{const} \end{equation}

2.2.2 Laplace-Runge-Lenz (LRL) Vector ($\vec{e}$)

While rotational invariance in any central force field $F(r)\hat{r}$ guarantees the conservation of $\vec{h}$, the inverse-square gravitational field $F(r) \propto r^{-2}$ possesses an additional hidden dynamical symmetry ($SO(4)$ for bound orbits) that prevents the orbit from precessing. This symmetry is encoded in the dimensionless Laplace-Runge-Lenz (LRL) vector (or eccentricity vector) $\vec{e}$.

Derivation from the Equation of Motion:

Take the cross product of the relative equation of motion $\ddot{\vec{r}} = -\frac{\mu_0}{r^2}\hat{r}$ with the constant angular momentum vector $\vec{h}$:

\begin{equation} \ddot{\vec{r}} \times \vec{h} = -\frac{\mu_0}{r^2}(\hat{r} \times \vec{h}) \end{equation}

Since $\vec{h}$ is constant ($\dot{\vec{h}} = \vec{0}$), the left-hand side can be written as a total time derivative:

\begin{equation} \ddot{\vec{r}} \times \vec{h} = \frac{d}{dt}(\vec{v} \times \vec{h}) \end{equation}

On the right-hand side, substitute $\vec{h} = r^2\dot{\theta}\hat{k}$ (or equivalently expand the vector triple product $\hat{r} \times (\vec{r} \times \dot{\vec{r}})$). Using the polar unit vector derivative identity $\frac{d\hat{r}}{dt} = \dot{\theta}\hat{\theta} = \frac{\vec{h}}{r^2} \times \hat{r} = -\frac{1}{r^2}(\hat{r} \times \vec{h})$:

\begin{equation} -\frac{\mu_0}{r^2}(\hat{r} \times \vec{h}) = \mu_0 \frac{d\hat{r}}{dt} \end{equation}

(Alternatively, in coordinate-free vector algebra: $\hat{r} \times (\vec{r} \times \dot{\vec{r}}) = \frac{1}{r}\left[(\vec{r}\cdot\dot{\vec{r}})\vec{r} - r^2\dot{\vec{r}}\right] = \dot{r}\vec{r} - r\dot{\vec{r}} = -r^2\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\vec{r}}{r}\right) = -r^2\frac{d\hat{r}}{dt}$.)

Equating the left- and right-hand sides gives:

\begin{equation} \frac{d}{dt}(\vec{v} \times \vec{h}) = \mu_0 \frac{d\hat{r}}{dt} \implies \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\vec{v} \times \vec{h}}{\mu_0} - \hat{r}\right) = \vec{0} \end{equation}

Integrating with respect to time defines the conserved Laplace-Runge-Lenz eccentricity vector $\vec{e}$:

\begin{equation} \vec{e} \equiv \frac{\vec{v} \times \vec{h}}{\mu_0} - \hat{r} = \mathrm{const} \end{equation}

Geometric Interpretation and Magnitude Proof:

  1. Coplanarity: Because $\vec{v} \times \vec{h}$ is perpendicular to $\vec{h}$ and $\hat{r}$ lies in the orbital plane (perpendicular to $\vec{h}$), $\vec{e} \cdot \vec{h} = 0$. Thus, $\vec{e}$ lies entirely within the orbital plane.
  2. Direction and Polar Orbit Equation: Let $\theta$ be the polar angle in the orbital plane and $\theta_0$ be the fixed angular direction of $\vec{e}$, so that the angle between $\vec{r}$ and $\vec{e}$ is $f = \theta - \theta_0$. Taking the scalar dot product of $\vec{r}$ with $\vec{e}$ yields:
    \begin{equation} \vec{r} \cdot \vec{e} = \vec{r} \cdot \left(\frac{\vec{v} \times \vec{h}}{\mu_0}\right) - \vec{r} \cdot \hat{r} \end{equation}
    Using the cyclic invariance of the scalar triple product, $\vec{r} \cdot (\vec{v} \times \vec{h}) = (\vec{r} \times \vec{v}) \cdot \vec{h} = \vec{h} \cdot \vec{h} = h^2$, and noting that $\vec{r} \cdot \vec{e} = r e \cos(\theta - \theta_0)$ and $\vec{r} \cdot \hat{r} = r$, we obtain:
    \begin{equation} r e \cos(\theta - \theta_0) = \frac{h^2}{\mu_0} - r \implies r(\theta) = \frac{h^2 / \mu_0}{1 + e\cos(\theta - \theta_0)} \end{equation}
    Since the denominator $1 + e\cos(\theta - \theta_0)$ is maximized—and the radial distance $r(\theta)$ is minimized—when $\cos(\theta - \theta_0) = +1$ ($\theta = \theta_0$), the vector $\vec{e}$ points strictly from the focus toward periapsis (the point of closest approach), and its magnitude $e = |\vec{e}|$ is the orbital eccentricity.
  3. Relation Between Energy, Angular Momentum, and Eccentricity: Squaring the LRL vector ($\vec{e} \cdot \vec{e} = e^2$) links $e$ directly to the specific mechanical energy $\epsilon = \frac{1}{2}v^2 - \frac{\mu_0}{r}$:
    \begin{equation} e^2 = \left(\frac{\vec{v} \times \vec{h}}{\mu_0} - \hat{r}\right) \cdot \left(\frac{\vec{v} \times \vec{h}}{\mu_0} - \hat{r}\right) = \frac{|\vec{v} \times \vec{h}|^2}{\mu_0^2} - \frac{2}{\mu_0}\hat{r}\cdot(\vec{v}\times\vec{h}) + |\hat{r}|^2 \end{equation}
    Since $\vec{v} \perp \vec{h}$, we have $|\vec{v} \times \vec{h}|^2 = v^2 h^2$, and $\hat{r}\cdot(\vec{v}\times\vec{h}) = \frac{1}{r}(\vec{r}\times\vec{v})\cdot\vec{h} = \frac{h^2}{r}$. Substituting these gives:
    \begin{equation} e^2 = \frac{v^2 h^2}{\mu_0^2} - \frac{2h^2}{\mu_0 r} + 1 = 1 + \frac{2h^2}{\mu_0^2}\left(\frac{1}{2}v^2 - \frac{\mu_0}{r}\right) = 1 + \frac{2\epsilon h^2}{\mu_0^2} \implies e = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2\epsilon h^2}{\mu_0^2}} \end{equation}

2.3 The Orbit Equation (Derivation)

While the scalar product $\vec{r} \cdot \vec{e}$ provides an algebraic route to the orbit equation, the differential calculus derivation via Binet's Equation is essential in Olympiad astrophysics because it generalizes to arbitrary central forces (such as general relativistic $r^{-3}$ perturbations or $J_2$ oblateness corrections).

2.3.1 Binet's Equation

In planar polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$, the radial and azimuthal components of the equation of motion $\ddot{\vec{r}} = -\frac{\mu_0}{r^2}\hat{r}$ are:

\begin{align} \text{Radial:} \quad & \ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2 = -\frac{\mu_0}{r^2} \label{eq:radial_polar} \\ \text{Azimuthal:} \quad & r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta} = \frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dt}(r^2\dot{\theta}) = 0 \implies r^2\dot{\theta} = h = \mathrm{const} \label{eq:azimuthal_polar} \end{align}

To eliminate time $t$ and solve for the geometric trajectory $r(\theta)$, introduce the reciprocal radial variable:

\begin{equation} u(\theta) \equiv \frac{1}{r(\theta)} \implies r = \frac{1}{u} \end{equation}

Using the angular momentum conservation law $\dot{\theta} = \frac{h}{r^2} = h u^2$, the time derivative operator $\frac{d}{dt}$ transforms into a spatial angular derivative operator $\frac{d}{d\theta}$ via the chain rule:

\begin{equation} \frac{d}{dt} = \dot{\theta}\frac{d}{d\theta} = h u^2 \frac{d}{d\theta} \end{equation}

First and Second Radial Time Derivatives:

  1. Differentiating $r = u^{-1}$ once with respect to time $t$ yields the radial velocity $\dot{r}$:
    \begin{equation} \dot{r} = \frac{dr}{dt} = \frac{dr}{du}\frac{du}{d\theta}\dot{\theta} = \left(-\frac{1}{u^2}\right)\frac{du}{d\theta}(h u^2) = -h \frac{du}{d\theta} \end{equation}
  2. Differentiating $\dot{r}$ a second time with respect to $t$ yields the radial acceleration component $\ddot{r}$:
    \begin{equation} \ddot{r} = \frac{d}{dt}\left(-h\frac{du}{d\theta}\right) = h u^2 \frac{d}{d\theta}\left(-h\frac{du}{d\theta}\right) = -h^2 u^2 \frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} \end{equation}

Meanwhile, the centripetal acceleration term $r\dot{\theta}^2$ in terms of $u$ becomes:

\begin{equation} r\dot{\theta}^2 = \left(\frac{1}{u}\right)(h u^2)^2 = h^2 u^3 \end{equation}

Substituting $\ddot{r}$, $r\dot{\theta}^2$, and $r^{-2} = u^2$ into the radial equation of motion $\eqref{eq:radial_polar}$ gives:

\begin{equation} -h^2 u^2 \frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} - h^2 u^3 = -\mu_0 u^2 \end{equation}

Dividing through by $-h^2 u^2$ (for non-rectilinear orbits where $h \neq 0$ and $u \neq 0$) produces Binet's Differential Equation for Keplerian motion:

\begin{equation} \frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} + u = \frac{\mu_0}{h^2} \label{eq:binet_kepler} \end{equation}

(Note: For a general central force per unit mass $f(r)\hat{r}$, Binet's equation takes the universal form $\frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} + u = -\frac{f(1/u)}{h^2 u^2}$.)

2.3.2 Polar Equation for Conic Sections

Binet's equation $\eqref{eq:binet_kepler}$ is a second-order linear inhomogeneous ordinary differential equation with constant coefficients, mathematically identical to a simple harmonic oscillator subject to a constant driving offset.

Step 1: Homogeneous and Particular Solutions

  • Particular Solution ($u_p$): Since the right-hand side $\frac{\mu_0}{h^2}$ is constant, a constant trial solution $u_p = C$ gives:
    \begin{equation} 0 + u_p = \frac{\mu_0}{h^2} \implies u_p = \frac{\mu_0}{h^2} \end{equation}
  • Homogeneous Solution ($u_h$): The homogeneous equation $\frac{d^2 u_h}{d\theta^2} + u_h = 0$ has the general harmonic solution:
    \begin{equation} u_h(\theta) = A\cos(\theta - \theta_0) \end{equation}
    where $A \ge 0$ and $\theta_0$ are the two integration constants determined by boundary conditions.

Combining both solutions gives the general solution for $u(\theta) = \frac{1}{r(\theta)}$:

\begin{equation} u(\theta) = \frac{1}{r(\theta)} = \frac{\mu_0}{h^2} + A\cos(\theta - \theta_0) = \frac{\mu_0}{h^2}\left[1 + \left(\frac{A h^2}{\mu_0}\right)\cos(\theta - \theta_0)\right] \end{equation}

Step 2: Identifying the Orbital Parameters

Define the dimensionless constant $e \equiv \frac{A h^2}{\mu_0} \ge 0$ (the eccentricity) and the characteristic length scale $p$ (the semi-latus rectum or parameter of the conic):

\begin{equation} p \equiv \frac{h^2}{\mu_0} = \frac{h^2}{G(m_1 + m_2)} \end{equation}

Inverting $u(\theta)$ yields the Polar Equation for Conic Sections (establishing Kepler's First Law generalized to all conic trajectories):

\begin{equation} r(\theta) = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos(\theta - \theta_0)} = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos f} \end{equation}

where $f \equiv \theta - \theta_0$ is the true anomaly (the angular displacement measured from periapsis $\theta_0$).

Geometric Interpretation of $p$ and Classification of Conic Orbits:

Evaluating $r(f)$ at $f = \pm \frac{\pi}{2}$ (perpendicular to the major axis through the focus) gives $r(\pm \pi/2) = p$. For elliptic ($0 \le e < 1$) and hyperbolic ($e > 1$) orbits of semi-major axis $a$, evaluating periapsis $r_p = \frac{p}{1+e}$ and apoapsis/asymptotic vertex $r_a = \frac{p}{|1-e|}$ connects $p$ directly to $a$ and $e$:

\begin{equation} p = \frac{h^2}{\mu_0} = a|1 - e^2| = \begin{cases} a(1 - e^2) & \text{for ellipses } (0 \le e < 1) \\ 2q & \text{for parabolas } (e = 1, \text{ periapsis } q = r_p) \\ |a|(e^2 - 1) & \text{for hyperbolas } (e > 1) \end{cases} \end{equation}
