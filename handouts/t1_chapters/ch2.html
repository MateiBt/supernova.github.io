Chapter 2 · The Unperturbed Two-Body Problem

2.1 Two-Body Problem Reduction

In Chapter 1, we cheated slightly: we assumed the central star $M$ was bolted rigidly to the origin while the smaller planet $m$ orbited around it. That is a great approximation when the Sun is $333\,000$ times heavier than the Earth, but it fails completely for binary stars, binary black holes, or even the Pluto–Charon system. In reality, both bodies pull on each other and both move.

At first glance, tracking two moving masses in 3D space looks like a six-degree-of-freedom nightmare. Fortunately, by exploiting the symmetries of Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation, we can split this coupled two-body system into two much simpler pieces: the trivial straight-line motion of their common center of mass, and the motion of a single fictitious particle orbiting a fixed center.

2.1.1 Center of Mass (Barycenter) Frame

Let us consider two point masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ (or spherically symmetric bodies, by the Shell Theorem) located at position vectors $\vec{r}_1$ and $\vec{r}_2$ in an arbitrary inertial reference frame. To track where $m_1$ is relative to $m_2$, we need a vector connecting them. Thus we define:

\[ \vec{r} = \vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2, \qquad r = |\vec{r}| = |\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2| \]

Here, $\vec{r}$ is the relative separation vector pointing from $m_2$ (usually chosen as the primary body) toward $m_1$ (the secondary body), and $\hat{e}_r = \frac{\vec{r}}{r}$ is the unit vector pointing along that line.

By Newton's Second Law and Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation, the equations of motion for the two masses in our inertial frame are:

\[ \begin{aligned} m_1 \ddot{\vec{r}}_1 &= -\frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^2}\hat{e}_r = -\frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^3}(\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2) \\ m_2 \ddot{\vec{r}}_2 &= +\frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^2}\hat{e}_r = +\frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^3}(\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2) \end{aligned} \]

Notice that the two gravitational forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction (Newton's Third Law). Adding these two equations together cancels the internal gravitational forces completely:

\[ m_1 \ddot{\vec{r}}_1 + m_2 \ddot{\vec{r}}_2 = \vec{0} \implies \frac{d^2}{dt^2}\left(m_1 \vec{r}_1 + m_2 \vec{r}_2\right) = \vec{0} \]

Just like with linear momentum conservation in basic mechanics, seeing a second time derivative equal to zero tells us that a weighted combination of the positions moves at constant velocity. Thus we define the position vector of the center of mass (barycenter) $\vec{R}_{\mathrm{cm}}$ as the mass-weighted average of $\vec{r}_1$ and $\vec{r}_2$:

\[ \vec{R}_{\mathrm{cm}} \equiv \frac{m_1 \vec{r}_1 + m_2 \vec{r}_2}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{m_1 \vec{r}_1 + m_2 \vec{r}_2}{M} \]

where $M = m_1 + m_2$ is the total mass of the binary system. Because $\ddot{\vec{R}}_{\mathrm{cm}} = \vec{0}$, the barycenter drifts through space at a constant velocity $\vec{V}_{\mathrm{cm}} = \dot{\vec{R}}_{\mathrm{cm}} = \text{constant}$. That means the barycenter itself is a valid inertial reference frame!

Let's shift our origin right onto the barycenter ($\vec{R}_{\mathrm{cm}} = \vec{0}$). In this barycentric reference frame, we denote the position vectors of the two bodies with primes: $\vec{r}_1' = \vec{r}_1 - \vec{R}_{\mathrm{cm}}$ and $\vec{r}_2' = \vec{r}_2 - \vec{R}_{\mathrm{cm}}$. By definition, these primed vectors satisfy two simple linear equations:

\[ m_1 \vec{r}_1' + m_2 \vec{r}_2' = \vec{0} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \vec{r}_1' - \vec{r}_2' = \vec{r} \]

Solving this $2 \times 2$ linear system expresses each body's barycentric position vector purely in terms of the relative separation vector $\vec{r}$:

\[ \begin{aligned} \vec{r}_1' &= +\left(\frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\right)\vec{r} = +\frac{m_2}{M}\vec{r} \\ \vec{r}_2' &= -\left(\frac{m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\right)\vec{r} = -\frac{m_1}{M}\vec{r} \end{aligned} \]

This is an important result. Once we know the relative vector $\vec{r}(t)$, we immediately know where both stars are in space:

  • Opposite Phase: The vectors $\vec{r}_1'$ and $\vec{r}_2'$ have opposite signs, meaning the two bodies always lie on opposite sides of the barycenter along a straight line passing through it.
  • Inverse Mass Lever Arm: Taking magnitudes gives $m_1 r_1' = m_2 r_2'$ and $r_1' + r_2' = r$. Basically, the heavier star hugs the barycenter closely, while the lighter star swings wide.
  • Homothetic Orbits: Because $\vec{r}_1'(t)$ and $\vec{r}_2'(t)$ are just constant scalar multiples of $\vec{r}(t)$, both bodies trace out geometrically similar conic sections with the exact same eccentricity $e$ and orbital period $T$, with semi-major axes $a_1 = \frac{m_2}{M}a$ and $a_2 = \frac{m_1}{M}a$ ($a_1 + a_2 = a$).

2.1.2 Reduced Mass & Equation of Relative Motion

Now let's find the differential equation for the relative separation vector $\vec{r} = \vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2$. Dividing the inertial equations of motion by $m_1$ and $m_2$, respectively, gives the individual accelerations:

\[ \ddot{\vec{r}}_1 = -\frac{G m_2}{r^2}\hat{e}_r, \qquad \ddot{\vec{r}}_2 = +\frac{G m_1}{r^2}\hat{e}_r \]

Subtracting $\ddot{\vec{r}}_2$ from $\ddot{\vec{r}}_1$ gives the relative acceleration $\ddot{\vec{r}} = \ddot{\vec{r}}_1 - \ddot{\vec{r}}_2$:

\[ \ddot{\vec{r}} = -\frac{G(m_1 + m_2)}{r^2}\hat{e}_r \]

Look closely at that equation: it has the exact same mathematical form as a test mass orbiting a fixed star, except the central mass is now the sum of both masses, $M = m_1 + m_2$! Thus we define two fundamental quantities:

  1. The Reduced Mass ($\mu$):
    \[ \mu \equiv \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{m_1 m_2}{M} \implies \frac{1}{\mu} = \frac{1}{m_1} + \frac{1}{m_2} \]
  2. The Standard Gravitational Parameter ($\mu_0$):
    \[ \mu_0 \equiv G(m_1 + m_2) = GM \]

Multiplying our relative acceleration equation by $\mu$ casts the two-body problem into an exact effective one-body Newton's Second Law:

\[ \mu \ddot{\vec{r}} = -\frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^2}\hat{e}_r \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \ddot{\vec{r}} = -\frac{\mu_0}{r^2}\hat{e}_r \]

Total Energy and Angular Momentum in the Barycentric Frame

Why did we define $\mu$ as $\frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}$? Let's check what happens when we compute the total angular momentum $\vec{L}_{\mathrm{tot}}$ and total mechanical energy $E_{\mathrm{tot}}$ of the binary in the center-of-mass frame ($\vec{v} = \dot{\vec{r}}$):

  • Total Angular Momentum: Substituting $\vec{r}_1' = \frac{m_2}{M}\vec{r}$ and $\vec{r}_2' = -\frac{m_1}{M}\vec{r}$ into $\vec{L}_{\mathrm{tot}} = m_1(\vec{r}_1' \times \dot{\vec{r}}_1') + m_2(\vec{r}_2' \times \dot{\vec{r}}_2')$ gives:
    \[ \begin{aligned} \vec{L}_{\mathrm{tot}} &= \left[m_1\left(\frac{m_2}{M}\right)^2 + m_2\left(\frac{m_1}{M}\right)^2\right](\vec{r} \times \vec{v}) \\ &= \frac{m_1 m_2(m_2 + m_1)}{M^2}(\vec{r} \times \vec{v}) = \mu(\vec{r} \times \vec{v}) = \mu \vec{h} \end{aligned} \]
  • Total Mechanical Energy: Similarly, substituting $\dot{\vec{r}}_1' = \frac{m_2}{M}\vec{v}$ and $\dot{\vec{r}}_2' = -\frac{m_1}{M}\vec{v}$ into the total kinetic plus potential energy yields:
    \[ \begin{aligned} E_{\mathrm{tot}} &= \frac{1}{2}m_1 |\dot{\vec{r}}_1'|^2 + \frac{1}{2}m_2 |\dot{\vec{r}}_2'|^2 - \frac{G m_1 m_2}{r} \\ &= \frac{1}{2}\mu v^2 - \frac{G M \mu}{r} = \mu\left(\frac{1}{2}v^2 - \frac{\mu_0}{r}\right) = \mu \epsilon \end{aligned} \]

which concludes the reduction. Every single formula from Chapter 1 carries over unchanged simply by replacing the central mass with $M = m_1 + m_2$ and multiplying specific quantities ($\vec{h}, \epsilon$) by the reduced mass $\mu$!

Idea: How to Avoid the #1 Binary Star Trap on Olympiads

Contestants frequently mix up the individual orbit radius $a_1$ with the total separation $a = a_1 + a_2$ in gravitational force equations. Always remember: the gravitational force depends on the full separation $r = r_1' + r_2'$ ($F = G m_1 m_2 / r^2$), whereas the centripetal acceleration of star $1$ in the barycentric frame depends on its smaller barycentric radius $r_1' = \frac{m_2}{M}r$. The safest workflow is to solve the problem first for the relative orbit of semi-major axis $a = a_1 + a_2$ using total mass $M = m_1 + m_2$ (for instance, $T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{G(m_1 + m_2)}a^3$), and then scale back to individual orbits at the very end via $a_1 = \frac{m_2}{M}a$ and $a_2 = \frac{m_1}{M}a$.

Worked Example 2.1: Astrometric Binary Mass & Eccentricity (4th IOAA 2010, Short Problem 4)

A visual binary star system lies at a distance $d = 10\text{ pc}$ from Earth, with its orbital plane perpendicular to our line of sight and an orbital period $T = 100\text{ years}$. The largest angular separation between the two components is $\theta_{\max} = 7.0''$ and the smallest angular separation is $\theta_{\min} = 1.0''$. If the semi-major axis of the primary star's barycentric orbit subtends an angle $\alpha_1 = 3.0''$, determine the orbital eccentricity $e$ and the mass of each star ($m_1$ and $m_2$) in solar masses ($M_\odot$).

Solution:
Because the orbital plane is face-on, the maximum and minimum angular separations correspond directly to apastron $\theta_{\max} = \theta_a = \frac{a(1+e)}{d}$ and periastron $\theta_{\min} = \theta_p = \frac{a(1-e)}{d}$ of the relative orbit. Thus the angular semi-major axis $\alpha = a/d$ and eccentricity $e$ are:

\[ \alpha = \frac{\theta_{\max} + \theta_{\min}}{2} = \frac{7.0'' + 1.0''}{2} = 4.0'', \qquad e = \frac{\theta_{\max} - \theta_{\min}}{\theta_{\max} + \theta_{\min}} = \frac{7.0 - 1.0}{7.0 + 1.0} = 0.75 \]

At distance $d = 10\text{ pc}$, an angle of $4.0''$ corresponds to a physical relative semi-major axis of $a = \alpha(\text{in arcsec}) \times d(\text{in pc}) = 4.0 \times 10 = 40\text{ AU}$. By Kepler's Third Law in solar units ($a$ in AU, $T$ in yr, $M$ in $M_\odot$):

\[ M = m_1 + m_2 = \frac{a^3}{T^2} = \frac{40^3}{100^2} = \frac{64\,000}{10\,000} = 6.4\,M_\odot \]

Since $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 = \alpha = 4.0''$ and $\alpha_1 = 3.0''$, the secondary's barycentric semi-major axis is $\alpha_2 = 1.0''$. Using the barycentric lever-arm relation $m_1 = \frac{\alpha_2}{\alpha}M$ and $m_2 = \frac{\alpha_1}{\alpha}M$, we obtain $m_1 = \frac{1.0}{4.0}(6.4\,M_\odot) = 1.6\,M_\odot$ and $m_2 = \frac{3.0}{4.0}(6.4\,M_\odot) = 4.8\,M_\odot$.

2.2 Conserved Vectors of the Motion

Because the equation of relative motion $\ddot{\vec{r}} = -\frac{\mu_0}{r^2}\hat{e}_r$ depends only on the standard gravitational parameter $\mu_0 = G(m_1 + m_2)$, it is much cleaner in celestial mechanics to work with specific (per unit reduced mass $\mu$) conserved vectors. In fact, an unperturbed Keplerian orbit is completely locked in place by two conserved vectors: $\vec{h}$, which fixes the orbital plane, and $\vec{e}$, which fixes the orientation and shape of the conic within that plane.

2.2.1 Specific Angular Momentum Vector ($\vec{h}$)

Just like in Section 1.2, we define the specific relative angular momentum vector $\vec{h}$ as the cross product of the relative position vector $\vec{r}$ and relative velocity vector $\vec{v} = \dot{\vec{r}}$:

\[ \vec{h} \equiv \frac{\vec{L}_{\mathrm{tot}}}{\mu} = \vec{r} \times \vec{v} = \vec{r} \times \dot{\vec{r}} \]

To see how $\vec{h}$ changes over time, we take its time derivative using the product rule for cross products:

\[ \frac{d\vec{h}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}(\vec{r} \times \dot{\vec{r}}) = (\dot{\vec{r}} \times \dot{\vec{r}}) + (\vec{r} \times \ddot{\vec{r}}) \]

Let's evaluate these two terms: the first term vanishes because any vector crossed with itself is zero ($\dot{\vec{r}} \times \dot{\vec{r}} = \vec{0}$), and substituting $\ddot{\vec{r}} = -\frac{\mu_0}{r^3}\vec{r}$ into the second term gives:

\[ \frac{d\vec{h}}{dt} = \vec{r} \times \left(-\frac{\mu_0}{r^3}\vec{r}\right) = -\frac{\mu_0}{r^3}(\vec{r} \times \vec{r}) = \vec{0} \implies \vec{h} = \text{constant vector} \]

Because $\vec{h} = \vec{r} \times \vec{v}$ is perpendicular to both $\vec{r}$ and $\vec{v}$ ($\vec{r} \cdot \vec{h} = 0$ and $\vec{v} \cdot \vec{h} = 0$), the relative trajectory $\vec{r}(t)$ and both individual orbits $\vec{r}_1'(t), \vec{r}_2'(t)$ are forever confined to a single fixed plane—the invariant orbital plane—with unit normal $\hat{k} = \vec{h}/h$. In planar polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ within that plane:

\[ \vec{h} = (r\hat{e}_r) \times (\dot{r}\hat{e}_r + r\dot{\theta}\hat{e}_\theta) = r^2\dot{\theta}\hat{k} \implies h = r^2\dot{\theta} = 2\frac{dA}{dt} = \text{constant} \]

2.2.2 Laplace-Runge-Lenz (LRL) Vector ($\vec{e}$)

Conservation of angular momentum $\vec{h}$ holds for any central force $F(r)\hat{e}_r$ because of spherical rotational symmetry. However, as we saw in Problem 1.5, a generic central force causes the orbit's periapsis to precess! Why do orbits in a pure $1/r^2$ gravitational field close on themselves into perfect, non-precessing ellipses? Because the inverse-square law possesses a hidden dynamical symmetry[1] encoded in a second conserved vector: the dimensionless Laplace-Runge-Lenz (LRL) vector (or eccentricity vector) $\vec{e}$.

Derivation from the Equation of Motion

To construct this conserved vector, we take the cross product of the relative equation of motion $\ddot{\vec{r}} = -\frac{\mu_0}{r^2}\hat{e}_r$ with the constant vector $\vec{h}$:

\[ \ddot{\vec{r}} \times \vec{h} = -\frac{\mu_0}{r^2}(\hat{e}_r \times \vec{h}) \]

Let's evaluate both sides of this equation:

  1. Left-Hand Side: Because $\vec{h}$ is constant in time ($\dot{\vec{h}} = \vec{0}$), we can pull $\vec{h}$ inside the time derivative:
    \[ \ddot{\vec{r}} \times \vec{h} = \frac{d}{dt}(\vec{v} \times \vec{h}) \]
  2. Right-Hand Side: Recall from Section 1.1 that in polar coordinates, $\vec{h} = r^2\dot{\theta}\hat{k}$ and the radial unit vector rotates at rate $\frac{d\hat{e}_r}{dt} = \dot{\theta}\hat{e}_\theta$. Since $\hat{e}_r \times \hat{k} = -\hat{e}_\theta$, we have:
    \[ -\frac{\mu_0}{r^2}(\hat{e}_r \times \vec{h}) = -\frac{\mu_0}{r^2}\left(\hat{e}_r \times r^2\dot{\theta}\hat{k}\right) = \mu_0 \dot{\theta}\hat{e}_\theta = \mu_0 \frac{d\hat{e}_r}{dt} \]
    (Notice how the $r^2$ in $\vec{h} = r^2\dot{\theta}\hat{k}$ canceled the $1/r^2$ in Newton's law of gravity! That exact cancellation only happens for an inverse-square force.)

Equating the two sides and dividing by $\mu_0$ gives a total time derivative equal to zero:

\[ \frac{d}{dt}(\vec{v} \times \vec{h}) = \mu_0 \frac{d\hat{e}_r}{dt} \implies \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\vec{v} \times \vec{h}}{\mu_0} - \hat{e}_r\right) = \vec{0} \]

Thus we define the conserved Laplace-Runge-Lenz (eccentricity) vector $\vec{e}$:

\[ \vec{e} \equiv \frac{\vec{v} \times \vec{h}}{\mu_0} - \hat{e}_r = \text{constant vector} \]
[1] In typical physics fashion, the Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector was discovered by neither Laplace, Runge, nor Lenz! It was first derived in 1710 by Jakob Hermann and generalized by Johann Bernoulli, rediscovered by Laplace in 1799, used as a vector calculus example by Carl Runge in 1919, and applied to the quantum hydrogen atom by Wilhelm Lenz in 1924.

Geometric Meaning of $\vec{e}$ and the Orbit Equation

What does the vector $\vec{e}$ actually represent in physical space? Let's check its three fundamental properties:

  1. It lies in the orbital plane: Since $\vec{v} \times \vec{h}$ is perpendicular to $\vec{h}$ and $\hat{e}_r$ lies in the orbital plane, $\vec{e} \cdot \vec{h} = 0$.
  2. It points directly toward periapsis: Let $\theta_0$ be the fixed angle of $\vec{e}$ in the orbital plane, and define the true anomaly $f \equiv \theta - \theta_0$ as the angle between $\vec{r}$ and $\vec{e}$. Taking the dot product of $\vec{r}$ with $\vec{e}$ yields:
    \[ \vec{r} \cdot \vec{e} = r e \cos f = \vec{r} \cdot \left(\frac{\vec{v} \times \vec{h}}{\mu_0}\right) - \vec{r} \cdot \hat{e}_r \]
    Using the cyclic property of the scalar triple product, $\vec{r} \cdot (\vec{v} \times \vec{h}) = (\vec{r} \times \vec{v}) \cdot \vec{h} = \vec{h} \cdot \vec{h} = h^2$, and noting that $\vec{r} \cdot \hat{e}_r = r$, we get:
    \[ r e \cos f = \frac{h^2}{\mu_0} - r \implies r(f) = \frac{h^2 / \mu_0}{1 + e\cos f} \]
    Basically, the denominator $1 + e\cos f$ is largest—and the distance $r(f)$ is smallest—when $f = 0$ ($\theta = \theta_0$). Therefore, $\vec{e}$ points strictly from the focus toward periapsis, and its magnitude $e = |\vec{e}|$ is the orbital eccentricity!
  3. Its magnitude links $e$ to $\epsilon$ and $h$: Taking the dot product of $\vec{e}$ with itself ($e^2 = \vec{e} \cdot \vec{e}$) and using $|\vec{v} \times \vec{h}|^2 = v^2 h^2$ (since $\vec{v} \perp \vec{h}$) gives:
    \[ \begin{aligned} e^2 &= \left(\frac{\vec{v} \times \vec{h}}{\mu_0} - \hat{e}_r\right) \cdot \left(\frac{\vec{v} \times \vec{h}}{\mu_0} - \hat{e}_r\right) \\ &= \frac{v^2 h^2}{\mu_0^2} - \frac{2}{\mu_0}\hat{e}_r \cdot (\vec{v} \times \vec{h}) + 1 \\ &= 1 + \frac{2h^2}{\mu_0^2}\left(\frac{1}{2}v^2 - \frac{\mu_0}{r}\right) = 1 + \frac{2\epsilon h^2}{\mu_0^2} \implies e = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2\epsilon h^2}{\mu_0^2}} \end{aligned} \]
    which concludes the derivation.
Idea: Using the LRL Vector to Solve Impulsive Thruster Burns in Two Lines

Whenever a spacecraft at position $\vec{r}_0$ executes an instantaneous thruster burn $\Delta\vec{v}$ (so its velocity jumps from $\vec{v}_0$ to $\vec{v}_1 = \vec{v}_0 + \Delta\vec{v}$ while $\vec{r}_0$ stays fixed), brute-force geometry requires messy systems of equations to find how much the periapsis rotated. Instead, just evaluate $\vec{h}_1 = \vec{r}_0 \times \vec{v}_1$ and plug it straight into $\vec{e}_1 = \frac{\vec{v}_1 \times \vec{h}_1}{\mu_0} - \hat{e}_r$! The magnitude $|\vec{e}_1|$ immediately gives the new eccentricity, and the components $(e_{1x}, e_{1y})$ immediately tell you the exact direction of the new periapsis $\tan\theta_0 = e_{1y}/e_{1x}$.

Chapter 2 · The Unperturbed Two-Body Problem

2.3 The Orbit Equation (Derivation)

In Section 2.2, we found the polar equation of a Keplerian orbit algebraically by dotting $\vec{r}$ into the Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector $\vec{e}$. While that vector trick is slick, you also need to master the differential calculus derivation via Binet's Equation. Why? Because if an Olympiad problem adds a general-relativistic $1/r^3$ force, a $J_2$ planetary oblateness term, or a power-law potential, the LRL vector is no longer constant—whereas Binet's differential equation works for any central force!

2.3.1 Binet's Equation

Let's write out the radial and azimuthal components of the relative equation of motion $\ddot{\vec{r}} = -\frac{\mu_0}{r^2}\hat{e}_r$ in planar polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Radial:} \quad & \ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2 = -\frac{\mu_0}{r^2} \\ \text{Azimuthal:} \quad & r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta} = \frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dt}(r^2\dot{\theta}) = 0 \implies r^2\dot{\theta} = h = \text{constant} \end{aligned} \]

Right now, $r(t)$ and $\theta(t)$ are functions of time $t$, and the radial equation looks horribly nonlinear because of the $r\dot{\theta}^2 = h^2/r^3$ and $-\mu_0/r^2$ terms. To eliminate time $t$ and linearize the equation for the geometric path $r(\theta)$, we introduce the reciprocal radial variable[2]. Thus we define:

\[ u(\theta) \equiv \frac{1}{r(\theta)} \implies r = \frac{1}{u} \]

Using conservation of specific angular momentum $\dot{\theta} = \frac{h}{r^2} = h u^2$, we can convert any time derivative $\frac{d}{dt}$ into an angular derivative $\frac{d}{d\theta}$ via the chain rule:

\[ \frac{d}{dt} = \dot{\theta}\frac{d}{d\theta} = h u^2 \frac{d}{d\theta} \]

Evaluating the Radial Time Derivatives

Let's apply this operator twice to $r = 1/u$:

  1. First Derivative (Radial Velocity $\dot{r}$): Differentiating $r = u^{-1}$ with respect to $t$ gives:
    \[ \dot{r} = \frac{dr}{dt} = \frac{dr}{du}\frac{du}{d\theta}\dot{\theta} = \left(-\frac{1}{u^2}\right)\frac{du}{d\theta}(h u^2) = -h \frac{du}{d\theta} \]
    Notice that the $u^2$ factors cancel completely! That is the magic of the $u = 1/r$ substitution: $\dot{r} = -h\frac{du}{d\theta}$ is linear in $u$.
  2. Second Derivative ($\ddot{r}$): Differentiating $\dot{r} = -h\frac{du}{d\theta}$ a second time with respect to $t$ using $\frac{d}{dt} = hu^2\frac{d}{d\theta}$ yields:
    \[ \ddot{r} = \frac{d}{dt}\left(-h\frac{du}{d\theta}\right) = h u^2 \frac{d}{d\theta}\left(-h\frac{du}{d\theta}\right) = -h^2 u^2 \frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} \]

Meanwhile, the centripetal term $r\dot{\theta}^2$ in terms of $u$ is simply:

\[ r\dot{\theta}^2 = \left(\frac{1}{u}\right)(h u^2)^2 = h^2 u^3 \]

Substituting $\ddot{r}$, $r\dot{\theta}^2$, and $1/r^2 = u^2$ into the radial equation of motion $\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2 = -\frac{\mu_0}{r^2}$ gives:

\[ -h^2 u^2 \frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} - h^2 u^3 = -\mu_0 u^2 \]

Dividing both sides by $-h^2 u^2$ (for any non-rectilinear orbit where $h \neq 0$) produces Binet's Differential Equation:

\[ \frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} + u = \frac{\mu_0}{h^2} \]

This is an important result. By switching from $r(t)$ to $u(\theta) = 1/r(\theta)$, a nonlinear inverse-square differential equation has turned into the simplest linear equation in physics: a simple harmonic oscillator with a constant offset!

IOAA Note (General Central Force Binet Formula): If a particle moves under an arbitrary central acceleration $\vec{a} = f(r)\hat{e}_r$ (where $f(r) \lt 0$ for an attractive force), the exact same steps yield the universal Binet Equation: \[ \frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} + u = -\frac{f(1/u)}{h^2 u^2} \] Furthermore, the total squared speed $v^2 = \dot{r}^2 + r^2\dot{\theta}^2$ takes the compact Binet form $v^2 = h^2\left[\left(\frac{du}{d\theta}\right)^2 + u^2\right]$.
[2] Although named after Jacques Philippe Marie Binet (1812), the reciprocal radius substitution $u = 1/r$ was actually introduced by Isaac Newton in Book I, Proposition 11 of the Principia (1687) to prove geometrically that an ellipse around a focus requires an inverse-square force.

2.3.2 Polar Equation for Conic Sections

Solving Binet's equation $\frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} + u = \frac{\mu_0}{h^2}$ takes just two steps:

  1. Particular Solution ($u_p$): Since the right-hand side $\frac{\mu_0}{h^2}$ is constant, a constant particular solution is simply $u_p = \frac{\mu_0}{h^2}$.
  2. Homogeneous Solution ($u_h$): The homogeneous harmonic oscillator equation $\frac{d^2 u_h}{d\theta^2} + u_h = 0$ has the general solution $u_h(\theta) = A\cos(\theta - \theta_0)$, where $A \ge 0$ and $\theta_0$ are constants of integration.

Adding $u_p$ and $u_h$ gives the general solution for $u(\theta) = 1/r(\theta)$:

\[ u(\theta) = \frac{1}{r(\theta)} = \frac{\mu_0}{h^2} + A\cos(\theta - \theta_0) = \frac{\mu_0}{h^2}\left[1 + \left(\frac{A h^2}{\mu_0}\right)\cos(\theta - \theta_0)\right] \]

Comparing this with our LRL orbit equation from Section 2.2, we identify the dimensionless eccentricity $e \equiv \frac{A h^2}{\mu_0} \ge 0$ and the characteristic length scale $p$, called the semi-latus rectum (or conic parameter):

\[ p \equiv \frac{h^2}{\mu_0} = \frac{h^2}{G(m_1 + m_2)} \]

Inverting $u(\theta)$ gives the universal Polar Equation for Conic Sections (generalizing Kepler's First Law to all bound and unbound two-body trajectories):

\[ r(\theta) = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos(\theta - \theta_0)} = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos f} \]

where $f \equiv \theta - \theta_0$ is the true anomaly measured from periapsis ($r_p = \frac{p}{1 + e}$ at $f = 0$). Notice that when $f = \pm\frac{\pi}{2}$ (90° from periapsis), $\cos(\pm\pi/2) = 0$ and $r(\pm\pi/2) = p$: the semi-latus rectum $p$ is literally the half-width of the conic section measured perpendicular to the major axis right through the focus!

Connecting $r_p = \frac{p}{1+e}$ and $r_a = \frac{p}{|1-e|}$ to the semi-major axis $a$ links $p$ and specific energy $\epsilon$ across all four conic families:

\[ p = \frac{h^2}{\mu_0} = \begin{cases} a(1 - e^2) & \text{for circles } (e = 0) \text{ and ellipses } (0 \lt e \lt 1) \\ 2q & \text{for parabolas } (e = 1, \text{ periapsis } q = r_p) \\ |a|(e^2 - 1) & \text{for hyperbolas } (e > 1) \end{cases} \]

Worked Example 2.3: Interstellar Comet Hyperbolic Flyby (USAAAO 2023 Second Round, Q2)

An interstellar comet ("Scratcher Crab") enters the Solar System with speed at infinity $v_\infty = 29.8\text{ km/s}$ (equal to Earth's circular orbital speed $v_\oplus = \sqrt{GM_\odot / \text{AU}}$) and impact parameter $b = 1.00\text{ AU}$. Determine the comet's semi-latus rectum $p$, hyperbolic semi-major axis $|a|$, orbital eccentricity $e$, perihelion distance $r_p$, and the total deflection angle $\Delta\phi$ between its incoming and outgoing asymptotes.

Solution:
Let $r_\oplus = 1.00\text{ AU}$ so that $\mu_0 = GM_\odot = r_\oplus v_\oplus^2$. Since the comet approaches with $v_\infty = v_\oplus$ and impact parameter $b = r_\oplus$, its specific angular momentum is $h = b v_\infty = r_\oplus v_\oplus$, and its specific energy is $\epsilon = \frac{1}{2}v_\infty^2 = \frac{1}{2}v_\oplus^2$. Thus the semi-latus rectum $p$ and hyperbolic semi-major axis $|a|$ are:

\[ p = \frac{h^2}{\mu_0} = \frac{r_\oplus^2 v_\oplus^2}{r_\oplus v_\oplus^2} = 1.00\text{ AU}, \qquad |a| = \frac{\mu_0}{2\epsilon} = \frac{r_\oplus v_\oplus^2}{v_\oplus^2} = 1.00\text{ AU} \]

From $p = |a|(e^2 - 1)$ with $p = |a| = 1.00\text{ AU}$, we immediately find $e^2 - 1 = 1 \implies e = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$ (a rectangular hyperbola!), with perihelion distance $r_p = \frac{p}{1 + e} = \frac{1.00\text{ AU}}{1 + \sqrt{2}} = (\sqrt{2} - 1)\text{ AU} \approx 0.414\text{ AU}$.
The asymptotes ($r \to \infty$) occur where the denominator $1 + e\cos f_\infty = 0 \implies \cos f_\infty = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \implies f_\infty = \pm 135^\circ$. Thus the total angle swept between the incoming and outgoing asymptotes is $2f_\infty = 270^\circ$, corresponding to a velocity deflection angle of $\Delta\phi = 2f_\infty - 180^\circ = 90.0^\circ$!

Problem 2.6 · Recovering an Asteroid's Conic Elements at Opposition (Olympiad POTD) [3 pts]

An asteroid is observed at opposition while moving in a prograde heliocentric orbit in Earth's orbital plane ($\mu_0 = GM_\odot = 1.327 \times 10^{20}\text{ m}^3\text{s}^{-2}$, $1\text{ AU} = 1.496 \times 10^{11}\text{ m}$). At the instant of observation, its geocentric distance is $\Delta = 1.20\text{ AU}$, its apparent angular motion relative to the background stars is retrograde at rate $\omega_{\mathrm{obs}} = 0.30^\circ\text{ day}^{-1}$, and its measured line-of-sight Doppler velocity relative to Earth is $v_{\mathrm{los}} = +5.0\text{ km/s}$ (receding). Assume Earth moves in a circular orbit of radius $r_\oplus = 1.00\text{ AU}$ with speed $v_\oplus = 29.78\text{ km/s}$.

  1. Determine the asteroid's heliocentric distance $r$, heliocentric transverse velocity $v_\theta$, heliocentric radial velocity $v_r$, and total orbital speed $v$.
  2. Calculate the asteroid's specific angular momentum $h$, semi-latus rectum $p$ (in AU), specific energy $\epsilon$, semi-major axis $a$ (in AU), and eccentricity $e$.
  3. Using the polar conic equation, find its perihelion distance $r_p$ (in AU) and true anomaly $f$ at the instant of observation.
Hint: At opposition, the Sun, Earth, and asteroid are collinear, so $r = r_\oplus + \Delta = 2.20\text{ AU}$, $v_r = v_{\mathrm{los}}$, and the apparent retrograde angular speed is $\omega_{\mathrm{obs}} = \frac{v_\oplus - v_\theta}{\Delta}$!
Full Solution:

(a) At opposition, the Sun, Earth, and asteroid lie on a straight line, so the heliocentric distance is $r = r_\oplus + \Delta = 1.00 + 1.20 = 2.20\text{ AU} = 3.291 \times 10^{11}\text{ m}$. Converting $\omega_{\mathrm{obs}} = 0.30^\circ\text{ d}^{-1} = 6.06 \times 10^{-8}\text{ rad/s}$ and noting that retrograde apparent motion at opposition means $v_\theta - v_\oplus = -\omega_{\mathrm{obs}}\Delta$:

\[ v_\theta = v_\oplus - \omega_{\mathrm{obs}}\Delta = 29.78\text{ km/s} - (6.06 \times 10^{-8}\text{ s}^{-1})(1.795 \times 10^{11}\text{ m}) \approx 18.90\text{ km/s} \]

Since Earth's radial velocity is zero, $v_r = v_{\mathrm{los}} = +5.00\text{ km/s}$, and the total speed is $v = \sqrt{v_r^2 + v_\theta^2} = \sqrt{5.00^2 + 18.90^2} \approx 19.55\text{ km/s}$.

(b) The specific angular momentum, semi-latus rectum, specific energy, semi-major axis, and eccentricity are:

\[ \begin{aligned} h &= r v_\theta = (3.291 \times 10^{11}\text{ m})(1.890 \times 10^4\text{ m/s}) = 6.220 \times 10^{15}\text{ m}^2\text{s}^{-1} \\ p &= \frac{h^2}{\mu_0} = 2.915 \times 10^{11}\text{ m} = 1.949\text{ AU} \\ \epsilon &= \frac{1}{2}v^2 - \frac{\mu_0}{r} = -2.121 \times 10^8\text{ J/kg} \implies a = -\frac{\mu_0}{2\epsilon} = 3.128 \times 10^{11}\text{ m} = 2.091\text{ AU} \\ e &= \sqrt{1 - \frac{p}{a}} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{1.949}{2.091}} \approx 0.261 \end{aligned} \]

(c) The perihelion distance is $r_p = a(1 - e) = 2.091(1 - 0.261) \approx 1.546\text{ AU}$. From $r = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos f}$, we have $\cos f = \frac{p/r - 1}{e} = \frac{1.949/2.20 - 1}{0.261} = -0.437 \implies f \approx +115.9^\circ$ (positive since $v_r = +5.0\text{ km/s} > 0$, moving away from perihelion).

Problem 2.7 · Jovian Hyperbolic Slingshot & Scattering Angle (IPhO 1999, Theory Q3) [4 pts]

In the reference frame of Jupiter (mass $M$), a space probe of mass $m \ll M$ approaches from a great distance with initial speed $v'$ and impact parameter $b$ (the perpendicular distance between Jupiter and the incoming asymptote).

  1. Using Binet's solution and the LRL eccentricity formula, prove that the polar equation of the space probe's hyperbolic trajectory around Jupiter (with $\theta = 0$ at periapsis) is given by: \[ \frac{1}{r(\theta)} = \frac{GM}{b^2 v'^2}\left(1 + \sqrt{1 + \frac{2Eb^2}{G^2 M^2 m^3}}\cos\theta\right) \] where $E = \frac{1}{2}mv'^2$ is the probe's total mechanical energy in Jupiter's frame.
  2. Determine the asymptotic angles $\pm\theta_{\mathrm{asym}}$ where $r \to \infty$, and prove that the angle $\Delta\psi$ by which the probe's velocity vector is deflected by Jupiter's gravity satisfies: \[ \sin\left(\frac{\Delta\psi}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{e} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{bv'^2}{GM}\right)^2}} \]
Hint: Express specific angular momentum $h = b v'$ and specific energy $\epsilon = E/m$ in the conic formula $\frac{1}{r} = \frac{\mu_0}{h^2}(1 + e\cos\theta)$ with $e = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2\epsilon h^2}{\mu_0^2}}$. For part (b), note that $2\theta_{\mathrm{asym}} = \pi + \Delta\psi$, so $\cos\theta_{\mathrm{asym}} = -\sin(\Delta\psi / 2)$.
Full Solution:

(a) At $r \to \infty$, the probe has specific angular momentum $h = bv'$ and specific mechanical energy $\epsilon = \frac{E}{m} = \frac{1}{2}v'^2$. Substituting $p = \frac{h^2}{GM} = \frac{b^2 v'^2}{GM}$ and $e = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2\epsilon h^2}{G^2 M^2}} = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2(E/m)(b^2 v'^2)}{G^2 M^2}} = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2E^2 b^2}{G^2 M^2 m^2 (\frac{1}{2}mv'^2)}} = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2Eb^2}{G^2 M^2 m^3}}$ (where $2E = mv'^2$ is absorbed if $L = mbv'$) into $\frac{1}{r(\theta)} = \frac{1}{p}(1 + e\cos\theta)$ directly gives:

\[ \frac{1}{r(\theta)} = \frac{GM}{b^2 v'^2}\left(1 + \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{bv'^2}{GM}\right)^2}\cos\theta\right) \]

(b) The asymptotes ($r \to \infty$, $\frac{1}{r} \to 0$) occur at $\cos\theta_{\mathrm{asym}} = -\frac{1}{e}$. Since the incoming and outgoing asymptotes are symmetric about periapsis ($\theta = 0$), the total angle swept by the radius vector from $t \to -\infty$ to $t \to +\infty$ is $2\theta_{\mathrm{asym}} = \pi + \Delta\psi$, where $\Delta\psi$ is the turning angle of the velocity vector. Therefore, $\theta_{\mathrm{asym}} = \frac{\pi}{2} + \frac{\Delta\psi}{2}$, and:

\[ -\frac{1}{e} = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + \frac{\Delta\psi}{2}\right) = -\sin\left(\frac{\Delta\psi}{2}\right) \implies \sin\left(\frac{\Delta\psi}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{e} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{bv'^2}{GM}\right)^2}} \]
Problem 2.8 · [A] Cotes' Spirals & Exact Binet Orbits for an Inverse-Cube Force [5 pts]

Suppose a particle of unit mass with non-zero specific angular momentum $h > 0$ moves in a central force field consisting of Newtonian gravity plus an attractive inverse-cube force of arbitrary strength $\kappa > 0$:

\[ f(r) = -\frac{\mu_0}{r^2} - \frac{\kappa}{r^3} \]
  1. Using the general Binet equation $\frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} + u = -\frac{f(1/u)}{h^2 u^2}$ for $u(\theta) = 1/r(\theta)$, show that $u(\theta)$ satisfies the linear differential equation: \[ \frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} + (1 - \gamma)u = \frac{\mu_0}{h^2}, \qquad \text{where} \qquad \gamma \equiv \frac{\kappa}{h^2} \]
  2. Solve this differential equation explicitly for $r(\theta) = 1/u(\theta)$ (assuming initial condition $\dot{r} = 0$ at $\theta = 0$, $u(0) = u_0$) in all three physical regimes: (i) the sub-critical precessing regime ($\gamma \lt 1$), (ii) the critical algebraic spiral regime ($\gamma = 1$), and (iii) the super-critical plunging regime ($\gamma > 1$, where the $-\kappa/r^3$ attraction overwhelms the centrifugal barrier $h^2/r^3$).
Hint: Substitute $f(1/u) = -\mu_0 u^2 - \kappa u^3$ into Binet's equation. For part (b), note that $1 - \gamma$ is positive for $\gamma \lt 1$ (giving $\cos(\sqrt{1-\gamma}\,\theta)$), zero for $\gamma = 1$ (giving a quadratic parabola in $\theta$), and negative for $\gamma > 1$ (giving $\cosh(\sqrt{\gamma - 1}\,\theta)$).
Full Solution:

(a) Substituting $f(1/u) = -\mu_0 u^2 - \kappa u^3$ into the universal Binet equation gives:

\[ \frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} + u = -\frac{-\mu_0 u^2 - \kappa u^3}{h^2 u^2} = \frac{\mu_0}{h^2} + \frac{\kappa}{h^2}u \implies \frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} + (1 - \gamma)u = \frac{\mu_0}{h^2} \]

(b) With $\dot{r}(0) = -h u'(0) = 0$ and $u(0) = u_0$, the exact solutions in the three regimes are:

  • (i) Sub-critical regime ($\gamma \lt 1$, let $k \equiv \sqrt{1 - \gamma}$): The general solution with $u'(0) = 0$ is a precessing rosette conic: \[ r(\theta) = \frac{1}{\frac{\mu_0}{k^2 h^2} + \left(u_0 - \frac{\mu_0}{k^2 h^2}\right)\cos(k\theta)} \] whose periapsis advances by $\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{prec}} = \frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{1 - \kappa/h^2}} - 2\pi$ per radial period.
  • (ii) Critical regime ($\gamma = 1$, so $\kappa = h^2$): The $u$ term cancels the centrifugal barrier completely, leaving $\frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} = \frac{\mu_0}{h^2}$. Integrating twice with $u'(0) = 0$ gives an algebraic spiral that winds into the origin ($r \to 0$) as $\theta \to \infty$: \[ r(\theta) = \frac{1}{u_0 + \frac{\mu_0}{2h^2}\theta^2} \]
  • (iii) Super-critical regime ($\gamma > 1$, let $\lambda \equiv \sqrt{\gamma - 1}$): The equation becomes $\frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} - \lambda^2 u = \frac{\mu_0}{h^2}$, whose solution with $u'(0) = 0$ is a hyperbolic Cotes' spiral: \[ r(\theta) = \frac{1}{-\frac{\mu_0}{\lambda^2 h^2} + \left(u_0 + \frac{\mu_0}{\lambda^2 h^2}\right)\cosh(\lambda\theta)} \] which plunges into the central singularity $r \to 0$ ($u \to \infty$) exponentially fast as $\theta$ increases!
