Module 6 · Section 1

The Milky Way Galaxy

We live inside the Milky Way — a barred spiral galaxy roughly 50 kpc in diameter containing hundreds of billions of stars, vast clouds of gas and dust, and an enormous dark matter halo. Understanding our own Galaxy is both the starting point for all of extragalactic astronomy and a unique challenge: we cannot step outside to photograph it. Everything we know comes from interpreting light and radio waves from an observing platform embedded deep within the disk.

6.1.0 Introduction & Historical Models

The band of the Milky Way — that faint, luminous river crossing the night sky — has fascinated observers since antiquity. It was Galileo Galilei who first resolved it into individual stars using his telescope in 1610. But understanding its structure took another three centuries.

Herschel and Kapteyn: The Sun-Centred Models

In the 1780s, William Herschel attempted a map of the Galaxy by counting stars in 683 sky regions, assuming: (a) all stars have the same absolute magnitude, (b) stellar density is constant, (c) no interstellar extinction, and (d) he could see to the edges of the distribution. His conclusion: the Sun sits near the centre of a disk five times wider than it is thick.

A century later, Jacobus Kapteyn refined this picture using statistical star counts and distance moduli, producing the Kapteyn Universe — a flattened spheroid with the Sun some 650 pc from centre. Both models were wrong for the same reason: they ignored interstellar extinction.

Interstellar Extinction & Distance

When extinction \(A_\lambda\) is neglected, the naive distance estimate \(d'\) overestimates the true distance \(d\):

\[ d = 10^{(m_\lambda - M_\lambda - A_\lambda + 5)/5} = d' \cdot 10^{-A_\lambda/5} \]
Because \(A_\lambda \geq 0\), we always have \(d \leq d'\): extinction makes stars appear farther away than they are. Typical disk extinction: \(\approx 1\,\text{mag kpc}^{-1}\) at visible wavelengths.

Shapley and the Globular Cluster Revolution

Between 1915 and 1919, Harlow Shapley determined distances to 93 globular clusters using RR Lyrae variable stars and their period-luminosity relation. He found the clusters concentrated around a point in Sagittarius, which he rightly identified as the Galactic centre — placing the Sun far from the middle, roughly one-third of the way from centre to edge.

Did You Know?

Shapley's estimate of the Galactic diameter (~100 kpc) was too large by about a factor of two, because his RR Lyrae calibration had errors and he under-corrected for extinction. Kapteyn's model was too small; Shapley's was too large. Both erred because of dust.

The Zone of Avoidance

Shapley noted that globular clusters are completely absent within ±10° of the Galactic plane — the zone of avoidance. He incorrectly attributed this to tidal disruption. In reality, interstellar extinction within the disk is so severe (>30 mag toward the Galactic centre) that even intrinsically very bright objects simply cannot be detected at optical wavelengths.

6.1.1 Distance Measurement Methods

All structural knowledge of the Galaxy depends on knowing distances. Astronomical distance determination proceeds in a ladder: each rung is calibrated by the one below it, extending our reach step by step from the solar neighbourhood to the far edge of the disk.

1. Trigonometric Parallax

As Earth orbits the Sun, nearby stars appear to shift against the distant background by an angle \(p\) (the parallax angle). The distance is then:

\[ d = \frac{1}{p''}\;\text{pc} \]
where \(p''\) is the parallax in arcseconds. Ground-based observations reach ~30 pc reliably; Hipparcos extended this to ~300 pc; Gaia achieves useful parallaxes to several kpc.

2. Distance Modulus and Photometric Parallax

If the absolute magnitude \(M\) of a star is known (from its spectral type and luminosity class), and we measure its apparent magnitude \(m\) and estimate the intervening extinction \(A\), the distance follows from:

\[ m - M = 5\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{d}{10\,\text{pc}}\right) + A(d) \]
The distance modulus \(\mu = m - M\) (without extinction) or \(\mu_0 = m - M - A\) (extinction-corrected). Solving iteratively since \(A = k\,d\) where \(k \approx 10^{-3}\,\text{mag pc}^{-1}\).
Worked Example

A B0 main-sequence star (\(M_V = -4.0\)) has \(V = +8.2\). Ignoring extinction: \(d' = 10^{(8.2+4.0+5)/5} \approx 2800\,\text{pc}\). With \(k = 10^{-3}\,\text{mag pc}^{-1}\), iterating \(d = 2800 \times 10^{-kd/5}\) gives \(d \approx 1400\,\text{pc}\) — the naive estimate is off by a factor of 2!

3. Statistical (Secular) Parallax

The Sun moves at \(\approx 20\,\text{km s}^{-1}\) relative to nearby stars, covering ~4 au per year. For a star at angular distance \(\vartheta\) from the solar apex at distance \(r\), the apparent proper motion due to the solar motion gives:

\[ r \approx \frac{s\sin\vartheta}{\mu_A} \]
where \(s\) is the Sun's displacement and \(\mu_A\) is the apex-component of proper motion. Averaging over a sample removes peculiar motion contributions.

4. Moving Cluster Method

Stars in a gravitationally bound cluster move through space together, appearing to converge toward (or diverge from) a convergent point at infinity. From the angle \(\phi\) between the cluster and convergent point and the mean radial velocity \(\langle v_r \rangle\):

\[ d\,(\text{pc}) = \frac{\langle v_r \rangle \tan\phi}{4.74\,\langle \mu'' \rangle} \]
Applied to the Hyades: \(d = 46 \pm 2\,\text{pc}\), confirmed by Hipparcos (\(47\,\text{pc}\)). This distance then calibrated the Hyades main sequence.

5. Main Sequence Fitting

If one cluster's H–R diagram is plotted with absolute magnitudes (distance known), another cluster's apparent-magnitude main sequence can be shifted vertically until it overlaps, yielding the distance modulus directly. More precise than single-star spectroscopic parallax because it averages over many stars.

Did You Know?

The entire extragalactic distance ladder historically rested on a single cluster: the Hyades. The Hyades distance calibrated the Cepheid period-luminosity relation, which in turn calibrated Type Ia supernovae — our standard candles for measuring the expansion of the Universe.

6.1.2 Morphology of the Galaxy

Overall Structure

The Milky Way is a barred spiral galaxy with a disk diameter of ~50 kpc. The Sun resides about \(R_0 = 8\,\text{kpc}\) from the Galactic centre (IAU value: 8.5 kpc; modern best value ~8.15 kpc from S2 orbit), located ~30 pc above the midplane — inside the thin disk. The Galactic centre direction is in Sagittarius, marked by the compact radio source Sgr A*.

The Thin and Thick Disks

The disk has two major stellar components described by exponential density profiles:

\[ n(z,R) = n_0 \left( e^{-|z|/z_\text{thin}} + 0.085\, e^{-|z|/z_\text{thick}} \right) e^{-R/h_R} \]
where \(z_\text{thin} \approx 350\,\text{pc}\), \(z_\text{thick} \approx 1000\,\text{pc}\), and \(h_R > 2.25\,\text{kpc}\) is the radial scale length. The thin disk contains young stars, gas, and dust; the thick disk contains older, more metal-poor stars.

The luminosity density of the thin disk is better described by:

\[ L(R,z) = L_0\, e^{-R/h_R}\, \text{sech}^2(z/z_0) \]
where \(\text{sech}(z/z_0) = 2/(e^{z/z_0}+e^{-z/z_0})\) is the hyperbolic secant, \(z_0 = 2z_\text{thin}\), and \(L_0 \approx 0.05\,L_\odot\,\text{pc}^{-3}\).

The Galactic Bulge and Bar

The boxy bulge at the Galaxy's centre is not a simple spheroid but contains a prominent bar with a half-length of \(4.4 \pm 0.5\,\text{kpc}\), oriented at \(\phi \approx 44°\) to the Sun–centre line-of-sight. The surface brightness of the bulge follows a de Vaucouleurs (\(r^{1/4}\)) profile:

\[ \log_{10}\!\left[\frac{I(r)}{I_e}\right] = -3.3307\left[\left(\frac{r}{r_e}\right)^{1/4} - 1\right] \]
where \(r_e\) is the effective radius (encloses half the bulge light) \(\approx 0.7\,\text{kpc}\) at 12 µm, and \(I_e\) is the surface brightness at \(r_e\). This profile also describes elliptical galaxies.

The Stellar Halo and Globular Clusters

The stellar halo extends to ~50 kpc and contains >150 globular clusters plus old, metal-poor field stars (high-velocity stars with \(|v| > 100\,\text{km s}^{-1}\) relative to the LSR). The halo number density profile follows:

\[ n_\text{halo}(r) = n_{0,\text{halo}}\left(\frac{r}{a}\right)^{-3.5} \]
where \(n_{0,\text{halo}} \approx 4 \times 10^{-5}\,\text{pc}^{-3}\) and the scale length \(a\) is a few kpc. The effective radius for the halo \(r^{1/4}\) law is \(r_e = 2.7\,\text{kpc}\).

Stellar Populations and the Age–Metallicity Relation

Stars are classified into Population I (young, metal-rich, circular orbits in the disk; \(Z \sim 0.02\)) and Population II (old, metal-poor, eccentric orbits, halo/thick disk; \(Z \sim 0.001\)). The iron-to-hydrogen ratio:

\[ [\text{Fe/H}] \equiv \log_{10}\!\left[\frac{(N_\text{Fe}/N_\text{H})_\text{star}}{(N_\text{Fe}/N_\text{H})_\odot}\right] \]
Stars with \([\text{Fe/H}] = 0\) have solar iron abundance; negative values are metal-poor.
Did You Know?

The Sun orbits the Galactic centre once every ~230 million years — called a Galactic year. The Sun is about 20 Galactic years old. At the end of the previous revolution, the Carboniferous period on Earth had just ended.

6.1.3 Galactic Coordinates & Kinematics

The Galactic Coordinate System

Because the Galactic midplane is inclined at 62.87° to the celestial equator, a dedicated coordinate system is used. Galactic longitude \(\ell\) is measured eastward from the direction of the Galactic centre (\(\ell = 0°\)) along the Galactic equator. Galactic latitude \(b\) is measured perpendicular to the plane.

Transformation from equatorial to Galactic coordinates (J2000.0):

\[\begin{aligned} \sin b &= \sin\delta_\text{NGP}\sin\delta + \cos\delta_\text{NGP}\cos\delta\cos(\alpha - \alpha_\text{NGP})\\ \cos b\,\sin(\ell_\text{NCP} - \ell) &= \cos\delta\,\sin(\alpha - \alpha_\text{NGP})\\ \cos b\,\cos(\ell_\text{NCP} - \ell) &= \cos\delta_\text{NGP}\sin\delta - \sin\delta_\text{NGP}\cos\delta\cos(\alpha - \alpha_\text{NGP}) \end{aligned}\]
where \(\ell_\text{NCP} = 122°.93\).

The Local Standard of Rest (LSR)

The dynamical LSR is a fictitious point instantaneously co-located with the Sun but moving in a perfectly circular orbit. Its velocity components in the cylindrical Galactic coordinate system \((R, \theta, z)\) are: \(\Pi_\text{LSR} = 0,\ \Theta_\text{LSR} = \Theta_0,\ Z_\text{LSR} = 0\) where \(\Theta_0 = \Theta(R_0)\) is the circular speed at the Sun's galactocentric radius.

A star's peculiar velocity \(\mathbf{V} = (u, v, w)\) is its velocity relative to the LSR:

\[ u = \Pi - \Pi_\text{LSR} = \Pi, \quad v = \Theta - \Theta_0, \quad w = Z \]
The Sun's peculiar velocity: \(u_\odot = -10.0 \pm 0.4\), \(v_\odot = +5.2 \pm 0.6\), \(w_\odot = +7.2 \pm 0.4\) km s⁻¹.

Oort's Formulas for Differential Rotation

For nearby stars (\(d \ll R_0\)), a first-order Taylor expansion of the angular velocity \(\Omega(R)\) around \(\Omega_0 = \Omega(R_0)\) yields the celebrated Oort formulae:

\[ v_r \approx A\,d\,\sin 2\ell \]
Observed radial velocity varies as a double sine wave with Galactic longitude.
\[ v_t \approx A\,d\,\cos 2\ell + B\,d \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \mu \approx A\cos 2\ell + B \]
Observed proper motion is a double cosine plus a constant offset \(B\).

The Oort constants are defined by:

\[ A \equiv -\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\frac{d\Theta}{dR}\bigg|_{R_0} - \frac{\Theta_0}{R_0}\right), \qquad B \equiv -\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\frac{d\Theta}{dR}\bigg|_{R_0} + \frac{\Theta_0}{R_0}\right) \]
IAU/Hipparcos values: \(A = 14.8 \pm 0.8\), \(B = -12.4 \pm 0.6\) km s⁻¹ kpc⁻¹.

Key relations between Oort constants and Galactic parameters:

\[ \Omega_0 = A - B = \frac{\Theta_0}{R_0}, \qquad \frac{d\Theta}{dR}\bigg|_{R_0} = -(A+B) \]

The Tangent-Point Method and 21-cm Mapping

Neutral hydrogen emits at 21 cm (the spin-flip transition) and is essentially transparent to its own radiation, making it visible across the entire Galaxy. For lines of sight with \(0° < \ell < 90°\) or \(270° < \ell < 360°\), the maximum radial velocity occurs at the tangent point where \(R_\text{min} = R_0 \sin\ell\):

\[ v_{r,\text{max}} = \Theta(R_\text{min}) - \Theta_0\sin\ell \approx 2A R_0(1 - \sin\ell) \]
By measuring \(v_{r,\text{max}}\) at various \(\ell\), the rotation curve \(\Theta(R)\) can be mapped for \(R < R_0\).
Did You Know?

The 21-cm hydrogen line was predicted theoretically by Hendrik van de Hulst in 1944 and first observed in 1951. Its discovery transformed galactic astronomy by making the full disk of the Milky Way accessible for mapping.

6.1.4 Rotation Curve & Dark Matter

The Flat Rotation Curve

If most of the Galaxy's mass were concentrated inside the solar circle, Newtonian gravity predicts Keplerian rotation beyond \(R_0\): \(\Theta \propto R^{-1/2}\). Observations show instead that the rotation curve remains flat — \(\Theta(R) \approx \text{const}\) — out to the limits of measurement (>20 kpc). This was first demonstrated definitively for external spirals by Vera Rubin and collaborators in the late 1970s.

A flat rotation curve with \(\Theta = V = \text{const}\) requires the enclosed mass to grow linearly with radius:

\[ M_r = \frac{V^2 r}{G} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{dM_r}{dr} = \frac{V^2}{G} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \rho(r) = \frac{V^2}{4\pi G r^2} \]
The implied \(\rho \propto r^{-2}\) falls off much more slowly than the observed \(r^{-3.5}\) of the stellar halo — most of the mass is dark.

Dark Matter Halo Density Profiles

To reproduce both the flat outer rotation curve and the rigid-body inner rotation, a commonly used profile is:

\[ \rho(r) = \frac{\rho_0}{1 + (r/a)^2} \]
Constant density for \(r \ll a\) (consistent with rigid-body rotation); \(\rho \propto r^{-2}\) for \(r \gg a\) (flat rotation curve). But \(M_r \propto r\) diverges — the halo must be truncated at large \(r\).

The Navarro–Frenk–White (NFW) profile, derived from cold dark matter (CDM) N-body simulations, provides a "universal" halo profile:

\[ \rho_\text{NFW}(r) = \frac{\rho_0}{(r/a)(1 + r/a)^2} \]
\(\rho \propto r^{-1}\) for \(r \ll a\); \(\rho \propto r^{-3}\) for \(r \gg a\). Scale radius \(a\) and characteristic density \(\rho_0\) are fit to observations.

For the Milky Way, the dark matter halo may contain up to \(5.4 \times 10^{11}\,M_\odot\) within 50 kpc and \(1.9 \times 10^{12}\,M_\odot\) within 230 kpc — roughly 95% of the total Galactic mass.

Dark Matter Candidates

Two broad classes have been proposed: WIMPs (weakly interacting massive particles) — hypothetical non-baryonic particles predicted by supersymmetric theories; and MACHOs (massive compact halo objects) — white dwarfs, neutron stars, black holes, or brown dwarfs. Microlensing surveys towards the LMC detected a small number of MACHO events, but far too few to explain the full dark matter halo: white dwarfs contribute at most 10%, red dwarfs at most 6%.

Did You Know?

The Galactic magnetic field within the disk follows the spiral arms and has a typical strength of only 0.4 nT — about 100,000 times weaker than Earth's surface field. Yet its energy density is comparable to the thermal energy density of the disk gas, meaning it plays a significant role in the dynamics and structure of the interstellar medium.

6.1.5 The Galactic Centre & Sgr A*

Observing Through the Dust

The line of sight from the Sun to the Galactic centre passes through nearly the maximum possible column of interstellar dust, producing >30 magnitudes of visual extinction. Optical astronomy fails completely; observations must be made in infrared, radio, X-ray, and gamma-ray bands.

Mass Distribution in the Inner Galaxy

Near the Galactic centre, orbital velocities of stars rise steeply inward, inconsistent with the \(r^{-1.8}\) stellar density distribution expected from an isothermal stellar gas alone. This implies either an ultra-dense stellar cluster or a compact massive object.

The S-Stars and Black Hole Mass

The star S2 (or S0-2) has been monitored for >20 years and completes a full orbit in 15.2 years, with orbital eccentricity \(e = 0.87\) and pericentre at only 120 AU from Sgr A*. From Kepler's third law:

\[ M = \frac{4\pi^2 a^3}{GP^2} \approx 3.7 \times 10^6\,M_\odot \]
with \(a_{S2} = r_p/(1-e) = 1.4 \times 10^{14}\,\text{m}\) and \(P = 15.2\,\text{yr}\). This mass is confined within <2 AU — only a supermassive black hole fits. The Schwarzschild radius: \(R_S = 2GM_\text{bh}/c^2 \approx 0.08\,\text{AU} = 16\,R_\odot\).

The Sgr A Complex

The inner 8 pc contains three distinct radio structures: Sgr A East — a young supernova remnant shell; Sgr A West — an unusual ionized "mini-spiral" H II region; and Sgr A* — an unresolved compact radio point source, the actual SMBH. The circumnuclear molecular ring (inner radius 2 pc, outer 8 pc) orbits at ~110 km/s.

Accretion Luminosity Estimate

The accretion rate onto Sgr A* is estimated at \(\dot{M} \approx 10^{-3}\) to \(10^{-2}\,M_\odot\,\text{yr}^{-1}\). If matter falls from \(r_i \gg R_S\) to \(r_f = R_S\), the virial theorem gives a radiated luminosity:

\[ L = \frac{1}{4}\dot{M}c^2 \]
Independent of both black hole mass and radius. The minimum accretion rate to produce \(10^7\,L_\odot\) is only \(\dot{M}_\text{min} \approx 2.7 \times 10^{-6}\,M_\odot\,\text{yr}^{-1}\) — far below the observed rate, so the current relative quiescence of Sgr A* is puzzling.
Did You Know?

Sgr A* itself is remarkably quiet compared to active galactic nuclei of similar mass. However, it does produce X-ray flares once per day, reaching 160 times its quiescent luminosity. Fermi Bubbles — enormous gamma-ray lobes extending 25,000 light-years above and below the Galactic plane — suggest Sgr A* was much more active a few million years ago.

Key Derivations
Derive Oort's radial velocity formula \(v_r \approx Ad\sin 2\ell\) from differential Galactic rotation.

Setup: The Sun (at \(O\)) and star (at \(S\)) both orbit the Galactic centre \(C\) with angular velocities \(\Omega_0\) and \(\Omega(R)\). The relative radial velocity is the difference in projections of orbital velocities along the line of sight \(OS\):

\[ v_r = \Theta\cos\alpha - \Theta_0\sin\ell = R_0(\Omega - \Omega_0)\sin\ell \]

where we used the geometric relation \(R\cos\alpha = R_0\sin\ell\) from triangle \(\triangle OTC\).

Taylor expand \(\Omega(R)\) about \(R_0\) for nearby stars (\(d \ll R_0\)):

\[\Omega - \Omega_0 \approx \frac{d\Omega}{dR}\bigg|_{R_0}(R-R_0) \approx \frac{1}{R_0^2}\!\left(R_0\frac{d\Theta}{dR}\bigg|_{R_0} - \Theta_0\right)(-d\cos\ell)\]

Substituting \(R - R_0 \approx -d\cos\ell\) (from geometry, for \(d \ll R_0\)) and simplifying:

\[v_r \approx \left(\frac{\Theta_0}{R_0} - \frac{d\Theta}{dR}\bigg|_{R_0}\right) d\cos\ell\sin\ell = \underbrace{-\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\frac{d\Theta}{dR}\bigg|_{R_0} - \frac{\Theta_0}{R_0}\right)}_{A}\cdot d\sin 2\ell\]

Using \(\sin 2\ell = 2\sin\ell\cos\ell\). This confirms the double-sine dependence observed in radial velocity surveys of nearby stars. ∎

Show that a flat rotation curve (\(\Theta = V = \text{const}\)) implies a dark matter density \(\rho \propto r^{-2}\).

For a test particle in a circular orbit of radius \(r\), centripetal acceleration equals gravitational acceleration (assuming spherical symmetry):

\[\frac{V^2}{r} = \frac{GM_r}{r^2} \quad \Rightarrow \quad M_r = \frac{V^2 r}{G}\]

Differentiating with respect to \(r\):

\[\frac{dM_r}{dr} = \frac{V^2}{G}\]

Using the mass-continuity relation \(dM_r/dr = 4\pi r^2 \rho(r)\) for a spherically symmetric distribution:

\[4\pi r^2 \rho = \frac{V^2}{G} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \boxed{\rho(r) = \frac{V^2}{4\pi G r^2}}\]

The implied \(r^{-2}\) profile falls off far more slowly than the stellar luminosity (\(\propto r^{-3.5}\) in the halo), confirming that the mass responsible for the flat rotation curve cannot be in the form of known luminous matter. ∎

Derive the accretion luminosity \(L = \frac{1}{4}\dot{M}c^2\) for a black hole accreting from large radius.

Consider matter of mass \(M\) spiralling through an accretion disk from initial radius \(r_i \gg r_f = R_S\) (the Schwarzschild radius). By the virial theorem, the energy radiated equals half the change in gravitational potential energy:

\[E_\text{rad} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{GM_\text{bh}M}{r_f} - \frac{GM_\text{bh}M}{r_i}\right) \approx \frac{GM_\text{bh}M}{2R_S}\]

Substituting \(R_S = 2GM_\text{bh}/c^2\):

\[E_\text{rad} = \frac{GM_\text{bh}M}{2} \cdot \frac{c^2}{2GM_\text{bh}} = \frac{Mc^2}{4}\]

Taking \(L = dE/dt\) and \(\dot{M} = dM/dt\):

\[\boxed{L = \frac{1}{4}\dot{M}c^2}\]

This efficiency (\(\sim 6\%\) of rest-mass energy) is independent of the black hole mass and far exceeds nuclear fusion (~0.7%). Real accretion disk models give efficiencies from ~6% (Schwarzschild BH) to ~42% (maximally spinning Kerr BH). ∎

Derive Oort's constants \(A\) and \(B\) for a purely Keplerian rotation curve, and show this disagrees with observations.

For Keplerian rotation: \(\Theta(R) = \sqrt{GM/R} = \Theta_0\sqrt{R_0/R}\). Differentiate:

\[\frac{d\Theta}{dR}\bigg|_{R_0} = -\frac{\Theta_0}{2R_0}\]

Substituting into the definitions of \(A\) and \(B\):

\[A = -\frac{1}{2}\!\left(-\frac{\Theta_0}{2R_0} - \frac{\Theta_0}{R_0}\right) = \frac{3\Theta_0}{4R_0}, \quad B = -\frac{1}{2}\!\left(-\frac{\Theta_0}{2R_0} + \frac{\Theta_0}{R_0}\right) = -\frac{\Theta_0}{4R_0}\]

Therefore: \((A-B)/(A+B) = (\Theta_0/R_0)/({\Theta_0/2R_0}) = 2\). But the observed Hipparcos values give \((A-B)/(A+B) = 27.2/2.4 \approx 11\) — a large discrepancy. The rotation curve near the Sun is much flatter than Keplerian, confirming that significant mass exists outside \(R_0\). ∎
